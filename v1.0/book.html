Chapter One
The Man Who Shaped Modern Uganda
An Introduction

There are leaders who inherit stable nations and maintain the status quo. Then there are leaders who forge nations from the ashes of destruction, who take broken systems and rebuild them brick by brick, who look at a continent burdened by centuries of exploitation and say, with unwavering conviction, "We shall overcome." Yoweri Kaguta Museveni Tibuhaburwa belongs to this latter category — a leader whose impact on Uganda and the broader African continent cannot be measured merely in years of service but in the fundamental transformation of an entire nation.

Born on September 15, 1944, in the rolling hills of Ntungamo District in southwestern Uganda, Museveni's journey from the son of a cattle keeper to the longest-serving leader in Uganda's history is one of the most remarkable political narratives of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. His story is inseparable from the story of modern Uganda — a nation that, under his stewardship, has risen from the ashes of dictatorship, civil war, and economic ruin to become one of the fastest-growing economies in East Africa and a stabilizing force in a volatile region.

To understand Museveni is to understand Africa itself — its struggles, its aspirations, its resilience, and its unyielding determination to chart its own course. When he took power on January 26, 1986, Uganda was a shattered state. The economy had collapsed, infrastructure lay in ruins, the rule of law had been replaced by the rule of the gun, and hundreds of thousands of Ugandans had perished under successive dictatorships. Museveni inherited a nation that many in the international community had written off as a failed state. What he did next would defy those expectations.

Through a combination of pragmatic economic reforms, a relentless focus on stability and security, ambitious investments in education and health, and a foreign policy that positioned Uganda as a leader in East African integration and Pan-African cooperation, Museveni transformed Uganda into a nation that consistently ranked among the top performers in Africa in terms of GDP growth, poverty reduction, and human development indicators. Under his leadership, Uganda's GDP grew from less than $4 billion to over $60 billion. Poverty rates fell dramatically. Millions of children gained access to education through Universal Primary Education. HIV/AIDS, which had ravaged the nation, was brought under control through one of the most successful public health campaigns on the continent.

But Museveni's story is not merely one of economic statistics and policy achievements. It is the story of a leader who understood, from his earliest days as a guerrilla fighter in the forests of the Luwero Triangle, that the liberation of Africa required not just the overthrow of tyrants but the building of institutions, the nurturing of human potential, and the cultivation of a continental identity that could stand proud in the face of external pressures. It is the story of a man who, when he captured Kampala in January 1986 and delivered his famous inaugural address, spoke not just to Uganda but to the entire continent:

"This is not a mere change of guards. I think this is a fundamental change in the politics of our government." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, January 26, 1986

He was right. It was a fundamental change — one that would ripple across East Africa and the Great Lakes region, one that would shape the destiny of millions, one that continues to define Uganda's trajectory four decades later.

This biography seeks to capture the full arc of Museveni's life and career: the revolutionary who fought in the bush for five years with fewer than 100 fighters; the statesman who negotiated peace agreements and regional alliances; the economist who liberalized a shattered economy and attracted billions in foreign investment; the leader who, for better or worse, has remained at the helm of Ugandan politics for over four decades, navigating crises, making difficult decisions, and leaving an indelible mark on his nation and his continent.

It is written not as hagiography nor as critique but as an honest examination of a complex, decisive, and consequential leader — one who, whatever one's political views, cannot be ignored. For Yoweri Museveni has not merely served as president of Uganda; he has shaped Uganda, and in doing so, has shaped the narrative of modern Africa itself.

The chapters that follow trace the arc of his life from the pastoral hills of Ntungamo to the halls of power in Kampala, from the battlefields of the Luwero Triangle to the conference rooms of the African Union, from the economic crisis of the 1980s to the oil-rich, infrastructure-driven economy of the 2020s. Along the way, we encounter the ideas, the decisions, the compromises, and the vision that have defined one of Africa's most enduring and impactful presidencies.

Let us begin where all great stories begin — at the beginning.

A Note on Sources and Perspective

This biography draws on a wide range of sources, including government records, academic research, international reports, journalistic accounts, and the words of Museveni himself. It has been written with the understanding that any attempt to capture the life and career of a leader who has shaped a nation for over four decades must necessarily be selective — highlighting certain episodes, themes, and achievements while necessarily leaving others in shadow.

The perspective of this biography is one that recognizes both the achievements and the complexities of Museveni's leadership. It seeks to understand the context in which decisions were made, the constraints within which the president operated, and the results that were achieved. It does not shy away from the challenges and criticisms that Museveni has faced, but it places them within the broader context of his presidency and the remarkable transformation of Uganda under his leadership.

For Ugandans who lived through the dark decades of Amin and Obote, who remember the terror of the bush war, who witnessed the collapse of the economy and the destruction of their communities, the stability and progress of the Museveni era represent a profound achievement. For the international community, Uganda under Museveni has been a story of transformation — a failed state reborn, a shattered economy rebuilt, a devastated population given hope.

For the critics, the story is more complicated. They point to the consolidation of power, the suppression of political opposition, the challenges of corruption and governance, and the questions about succession and the future. These criticisms deserve serious engagement, and they are addressed in the chapters that follow.

But the fundamental story of the Museveni era — the story of a nation rebuilt, an economy transformed, and a people given the chance to build a better life — is a story that must be told. It is a story of leadership, of vision, and of the remarkable capacity of human beings to overcome seemingly insurmountable challenges.

This is that story.

The Structure of This Biography

The fifteen chapters of this biography are organized thematically as well as chronologically. The early chapters trace Museveni's journey from his birth in Ntungamo through his education, his revolutionary struggles, and his seizure of power. The middle chapters examine his achievements in governance — economic reform, stability and security, education and health, infrastructure, and foreign policy. The later chapters address the challenges and complexities of his long presidency, collect his most memorable words, and assess his legacy.

This structure reflects the dual nature of the Museveni story: it is both the story of a man and the story of a nation. To understand one is to understand the other. Museveni's personal journey — from cattle keeper's son to revolutionary to president — mirrors Uganda's own journey from colonial subject to independent nation to shattered state to developing economy. The two stories are intertwined, each shaping and being shaped by the other in ways that make them impossible to separate.

The quotes and speeches that are woven throughout the biography are not mere decorations. They are windows into the mind of a leader who uses language as a tool of governance — who understands that words can inspire, clarify, persuade, and sometimes intimidate. They are evidence of a leader who thinks deeply about the challenges he faces and who articulates his vision with a directness that is sometimes startling and always revealing.

Finally, a word about the tone of this biography. It has been written from the perspective that Yoweri Museveni is one of the most consequential leaders in the history of independent Africa — a leader whose impact on Uganda and the broader continent demands serious analysis and engagement. This does not mean that every decision he has made has been correct, or that every aspect of his presidency has been beyond reproach. It means that his story is one that matters — one that has shaped the lives of millions and one that will continue to shape the destiny of Uganda and East Africa for generations to come.

With this understanding, let us turn to the beginning.

Chapter Two
Birth, Childhood, and Education
The Forging of a Leader

On September 15, 1944, in the midst of the Second World War, in the rolling green hills of Ntungamo District in southwestern Uganda, a child was born who would one day reshape the destiny of an entire nation. Yoweri Kaguta Museveni Tibuhaburwa entered the world as the son of Mzee Amos Kaguta and Esteri Kokundeka Nganzi, two cattle keepers from the Hima ethnic group of the ancient Kingdom of Mpororo — a kingdom that once stretched across parts of what is now southwestern Uganda and northeastern Rwanda.

The Name "Museveni"

The story of the name "Museveni" itself carries the weight of history. His father, Amos Kaguta, served as a soldier in the King's African Rifles, the British colonial regiment, during the Second World War. He was attached to the 7th Battalion — a detail that would give his son a name. According to former Tanzanian President Julius Nyerere, when Yoweri was born, relatives observed that his father was "a mu-seven" — meaning "one of the seventh" battalion. This is how Yoweri acquired the name Museveni, a name that would later become synonymous with revolution, governance, and African leadership.

There is something poetically fitting about this origin. The very name of the man who would eventually overthrow the last military dictatorships in Uganda was rooted in service — military service, to be precise. Amos Kaguta's service in the King's African Rifles placed him among the thousands of East African soldiers who fought in the global conflict against fascism, and the tradition of service and sacrifice would be carried forward by his son in ways that neither father nor son could have imagined.

Childhood in Ntungamo

Yoweri grew up in the pastoral landscapes of Ankole, a region characterized by its rolling hills, fertile grasslands, and the cattle-keeping traditions of the Hima people. The Hima, a Nilotic-influenced group, were predominantly pastoralists, and the rhythms of cattle herding, seasonal migrations, and communal decision-making that governed life in Ankole would profoundly shape the young Museveni's worldview.

The Mpororo Kingdom, from which the Hima traced their lineage, had its own rich traditions of governance and political organization. The kingdom's systems of accountability, where leaders were expected to serve their people and could be held accountable for their decisions, would leave an imprint on Museveni's political philosophy — a philosophy that, decades later, would manifest in his insistence on grassroots democracy through village-level Resistance Councils.

Life in Ntungamo was not without its hardships. Uganda in the 1940s and 1950s was still under British colonial rule, and the effects of colonialism — land alienation, forced labor, heavy taxation, and the disruption of traditional social structures — were felt even in the remote hills of the southwest. Young Yoweri grew up witnessing these injustices firsthand, an experience that would later fuel his revolutionary fire.

His family later migrated from Ntungamo during the 1960s, a period of significant upheaval as Uganda moved from colonial rule to independence and the early years of nationhood. This migration exposed the young Museveni to different parts of Uganda and different ethnic groups, broadening his perspective beyond the Hima heartland of Ankole and giving him a more national — and eventually continental — outlook.

Education: Building the Mind of a Revolutionary

Museveni's formal education began in 1953 at Kyamate Boys School in Ntungamo, a modest primary school where the foundations of learning were laid. In 1959, he moved to Mbarara High School, one of the notable secondary schools in western Uganda, where he began to develop the intellectual curiosity and analytical skills that would serve him throughout his career.

However, it was at Ntare School — the prestigious secondary school in Mbarara that has produced some of Uganda's most prominent leaders — where Museveni truly came into his own. Admitted in 1961, Ntare School provided a rigorous academic environment and, more importantly, exposed him to a generation of young Ugandans who would go on to shape the nation's future. The school's emphasis on leadership, discipline, and public service resonated with the young Museveni, who was already developing the restless ambition and sense of purpose that would define his life.

After completing his secondary education, Museveni applied to Makerere University, then Makerere College, the oldest and most prestigious institution of higher learning in East Africa. But fate had other plans. Despite his qualifications, Makerere rejected his application on the grounds that he failed to meet the proof of citizenship requirements — a bureaucratic obstacle that would, ironically, redirect the course of Ugandan history.

Even the sitting president, Apollo Milton Obote, pleaded Museveni's case to Makerere. The university still declined. But Obote, recognizing the young man's potential, used his influence in Tanzania to secure Museveni's admission to the University of Dar es Salaam. It was a decision with consequences that would reverberate across the continent for decades.

The University of Dar es Salaam: The Crucible of Pan-Africanism

The University of Dar es Salaam in the early 1960s was one of the most intellectually vibrant institutions in Africa. It was a hotbed of Pan-African thought, anti-colonial activism, and revolutionary theory. Students from across the continent — from Mozambique, South Africa, Zimbabwe, and beyond — converged in Dar es Salaam, drawn by the university's reputation for progressive scholarship and its proximity to Julius Nyerere's Tanzania, which was itself a laboratory for African socialism under the philosophy of Ujamaa.

It was here that Museveni studied economics and political science under some of the most influential thinkers of the era. Chief among his mentors was Walter Rodney, the brilliant Guyanese historian and activist whose seminal work "How Europe Underdeveloped Africa" would become a foundational text of Pan-African economic thought. Under Rodney's influence, Museveni deepened his understanding of the structural forces that kept Africa poor and dependent — colonialism, neocolonialism, exploitative trade relationships, and the comprador class that served foreign interests at the expense of African development.

Museveni also immersed himself in the works of Frantz Fanon, the Martinican psychiatrist and revolutionary philosopher whose writings on violence, decolonization, and the psychology of oppression became central texts of anti-colonial movements across Africa. Museveni's university thesis — on the applicability of Frantz Fanon's ideas on revolutionary violence to post-colonial Africa — demonstrated his early engagement with the theoretical foundations of liberation struggle.

At Dar es Salaam, Museveni also formed the University Students' African Revolutionary Front, a student activist group that brought together like-minded young Africans committed to the liberation of the continent. The group led a student delegation to FRELIMO-held territory in Portuguese Mozambique, where the young revolutionaries received military training alongside the Mozambican liberation fighters. This experience — the first taste of actual armed struggle — would prove invaluable in the years ahead.

The Making of a Political Thinker

The years at Dar es Salaam were formative in every sense. Museveni emerged from the university not just with a degree but with a comprehensive political philosophy that combined Marxist economic analysis, Fanonian revolutionary theory, Pan-African idealism, and a pragmatic understanding of power. He understood that Africa's liberation required not just military struggle but intellectual preparation, not just political revolution but economic transformation.

He joined Uganda's intelligence service under President Obote in 1970, gaining firsthand experience in the murky world of intelligence gathering, covert operations, and political maneuvering. But this was not a man content to serve within existing structures. The young Museveni was already looking ahead, already planning the kind of fundamental transformation that would require going outside the system to change it.

When Idi Amin seized power in a military coup on January 25, 1971, Museveni's world — and Uganda's — was turned upside down. The coup, which overthrew Obote and installed one of Africa's most notorious dictators, would set in motion a chain of events that would eventually lead Museveni from the lecture halls of Dar es Salaam to the battlefields of liberation.

But that story — the story of revolution and war — belongs to the next chapter.

Chapter Three
The Bush War
Liberation Through Fire — The NRA Struggle, 1981–1986

The road to power in Uganda has never been smooth, and for Yoweri Museveni, the road ran through five years of bush war — through forests and swamps, through ambushes and firefights, through years of sleeping on the ground with fewer than 100 fighters, through the loss of comrades and the constant threat of annihilation. It was a crucible that tested everything he believed in, everything he had learned, and everything he aspired to be. And it was in this crucible that the leader who would transform Uganda was forged.

The Overthrow of Amin and the FRONASA

When Idi Amin seized power in January 1971, Museveni was already organizing resistance. As the overthrow of Obote became apparent, Museveni's group of left-leaning intellectuals allied with Obote's exiled loyalists to oppose Amin's regime. Based in Tanzania, they repeatedly crossed into Uganda to gather intelligence and carry out sabotage missions.

In August 1971, Museveni's force attempted to set up a guerrilla base on Mount Elgon, near the Kenya-Uganda border. The operation failed, but it demonstrated the young revolutionary's willingness to act — to put theory into practice, to risk everything for the liberation of his country.

In September 1972, a coalition of exile forces, including Museveni's group, invaded Uganda from Tanzania in an attempt to overthrow Amin. The invasion was repelled, and Museveni's group suffered heavy losses. But rather than being defeated, Museveni was energized. He broke away from the mainstream opposition and in 1973 formed the Front for National Salvation (FRONASA) — a disciplined, ideologically committed fighting force that would play a crucial role in the eventual overthrow of Amin.

In August 1973, Museveni married Janet Kainembabazi, a union that would prove to be one of the most stable and enduring partnerships in Ugandan political history. Janet Museveni, as she would become known, would become one of the most influential women in Ugandan politics — serving as Minister of State for Karamoja Affairs, Minister for Gender, Labour and Social Development, and Minister of Education and Sports.

The Tanzania-Uganda War and the Fall of Amin

In October 1978, Idi Amin made one of his most catastrophic miscalculations: he ordered the invasion of the Kagera Salient in northern Tanzania. The invasion was an act of imperial aggression that united the entire Tanzanian military against Uganda and gave President Julius Nyerere the justification he needed to mobilize a full-scale counter-offensive.

Nyerere mobilized Ugandan dissidents to fight alongside the Tanzania People's Defence Force (TPDF), and Museveni and his FRONASA troops were attached to Tanzanian forces under Brigadier Silas Mayunga. Museveni's forces fought in the Western Uganda campaign of 1979, and he was present during the capture of Mbarara in February 1979 — a city in the heart of his home region that held deep personal significance.

The combined Tanzanian-Ugandan rebel forces captured Kampala on April 11, 1979, and Amin fled into exile, first to Libya and then to Saudi Arabia, where he would die in 2003. The overthrow of Amin was a triumph — but it was only the beginning of a much longer struggle.

The Interregnum and the Return of Obote

The post-Amin period was characterized by political fragmentation and instability. The Uganda National Liberation Front (UNLF), a coalition of anti-Amin forces, governed the country provisionally from April 1979 to December 1980. Museveni played a significant role in the Moshi Conference, which led to the unification of the opposition under the UNLF umbrella, though he felt that his FRONASA was not granted enough representation in the resulting power-sharing arrangement.

In the 1980 general election, Museveni contested the presidency on the platform of the Uganda Patriotic Movement (UPM). But the election, widely considered to have been rigged, returned Milton Obote to power. Museveni claimed electoral fraud — a claim supported by many independent observers — and made a decision that would change Uganda forever: he would take to the bush.

The NRA: A Revolutionary Army is Born

On February 6, 1981, Museveni and his supporters launched the guerrilla campaign that would become known as the Ugandan Bush War. They formed the Popular Resistance Army (PRA), which was later unified with other anti-Obote groups to become the National Resistance Army (NRA), the armed wing of the National Resistance Movement (NRM).

The NRA began with a core of fewer than 100 fighters — a ragtag band of intellectuals, former soldiers, and idealists who set out from the bush of the Luwero Triangle, a densely forested region about 80 kilometers north of Kampala, to take on the might of Obote's Uganda National Liberation Army (UNLA). The UNLA, backed by Tanzania and North Korea, had at its disposal an army of over 15,000 soldiers equipped with heavy weapons and supported by helicopter gunships.

The odds were staggering. But Museveni had something that Obote did not: a revolutionary strategy rooted in the theories of guerrilla warfare he had studied at Dar es Salaam — the Maoist concept of winning the support of the population through political education and good conduct; the Fanonian understanding of the psychological dimensions of struggle; and the practical lessons of his years of organizing in exile.

The Luwero Triangle: People's War

The NRA's strategy in the Luwero Triangle was a masterclass in guerrilla warfare. Rather than seeking to confront the UNLA in conventional battle, Museveni's forces conducted hit-and-run operations, ambushing government convoys, attacking isolated military installations, and then melting back into the dense forests and the support of the local population.

Crucially, the NRA imposed a strict code of conduct on its fighters. Soldiers were forbidden from stealing from civilians, from harassing women, and from destroying property. This discipline — unusual for guerrilla armies in Africa — won the NRA the support and loyalty of the communities in the Luwero Triangle, who provided food, shelter, intelligence, and recruits.

The NRA also conducted political education programs in the areas under its control, teaching villagers about the principles of the National Resistance Movement and the kind of government the NRM would establish if it came to power. This combination of military action and political mobilization — what Mao Zedong had called "the fish and the sea" — was highly effective.

The UNLA, by contrast, responded to the NRA's insurgency with brutal repression. Operation Simsim and other military campaigns devastated the civilian population of the Luwero Triangle and other regions. Entire villages were burned, food crops were destroyed, and hundreds of thousands of civilians were killed. The Luwero Triangle, which had been one of Uganda's most productive agricultural regions, was reduced to a wasteland of mass graves and abandoned fields. By the end of the bush war, an estimated 100,000 to 500,000 people had perminated.

The Growth of the NRA

Despite the overwhelming forces arrayed against them, the NRA grew steadily. From fewer than 100 fighters in February 1981, the NRA had grown to approximately 900 fighters by December 1981. By early 1983, it had expanded to around 4,000 fighters. By late 1985, the NRA had approximately 10,000 fighters under arms.

This growth was driven not by external support — the NRA received relatively little international assistance — but by the internal dynamics of the conflict: the UNLA's brutality alienated the civilian population and drove recruits to the NRA, while the NRA's discipline and political message attracted young Ugandans who were disillusioned with Obote's government.

The NRA's leadership included some of the most talented military and political minds of their generation: Salim Saleh (Museveni's brother), Fred Rwigyema, Sam Magara, Steven Kashaka, Joram Mugume, and Pecos Kuteesa, among others. These men would go on to play crucial roles in Uganda's post-war government and military.

The Final Offensive

In July 1985, Obote was overthrown in a military coup by his own generals, Bazilio Olara-Okello and Tito Okello. The Okello regime attempted to negotiate peace with the various rebel groups but refused to make concessions to the NRA. Museveni, who had learned the lessons of the Moshi Conference, was not willing to accept a peripheral role in a new government.

From August to December 1985, the NRA launched a series of devastating offensives that swept through western and southern Uganda. Towns that had been under UNLA control fell one after another. The NRA's advance was characterized by its discipline — in contrast to the looting and destruction that had accompanied previous changes of power, the NRA's entry into captured towns was relatively orderly.

On January 26, 1986, the NRA captured Kampala in the decisive Battle of Kampala. Tito Okello fled to northern Uganda and eventually into exile. Museveni's five-year guerrilla campaign — fought with fewer than 100 fighters at the outset against an army of tens of thousands — had achieved its objective.

On January 29, 1986, Yoweri Kaguta Museveni was sworn in as the President of the Republic of Uganda. He was 41 years old. The revolution was over. The real work was about to begin.

Chapter Four
Taking Power
January 26, 1986 — The Speech That Defined a Generation

January 26, 1986, was not just a date on the calendar. It was a turning point — not only for Uganda but for the politics of the entire African continent. On that day, a man who had spent five years in the bush, who had started his revolution with fewer than 100 fighters, who had been written off by many as a marginal rebel with no realistic chance of capturing power, stood on the steps of the Ugandan Parliament and was sworn in as president. And then he delivered one of the most famous speeches in the history of independent Africa.

The Inaugural Address

The speech that Museveni delivered on January 26, 1986, is studied in political science courses across the world, not because of its rhetorical elegance but because of the clarity of its diagnosis. While many African leaders, upon taking power, spoke in vague platitudes about unity and progress, Museveni did something remarkable: he identified, with surgical precision, what he believed was the fundamental problem of African governance.

"This is not a mere change of guards. I think this is a fundamental change in the politics of our government. The problem of Africa in general and Uganda in particular is not the people but leaders who want to overstay in power — those who seek to serve their own personal interests rather than the interests of the people they lead." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, Inaugural Address, January 26, 1986

This was not merely a political speech. It was a manifesto, a diagnosis, and a promise — all compressed into a few powerful sentences. Museveni was telling Uganda, and by extension Africa, that the continent's problems were not rooted in its people, its culture, or its geography, but in its leaders — leaders who seized power and refused to leave, who used the state as a personal fiefdom, who prioritized their own interests over those of their citizens.

The speech was a direct repudiation of everything that had gone before: the one-party dictatorships of Obote, the military tyranny of Amin, the chaos of the post-Amin interregnum. Museveni was signaling that the NRM government would be different — that it would govern through institutions rather than individuals, that it would seek the consent of the governed rather than ruling by force, and that it would create the conditions for a new kind of politics.

Inheriting a Broken State

The scale of the challenge that confronted Museveni on January 26, 1986, can hardly be overstated. Uganda was a shattered state. The economy, which had been one of the most vibrant in East Africa at independence, had collapsed under the weight of Amin's mismanagement and the devastation of the bush war. The GDP had fallen to less than $4 billion. Inflation was rampant. The national currency, the Ugandan shilling, had lost most of its value. Infrastructure — roads, railways, power plants, water systems — had been destroyed or fallen into disrepair.

The social fabric of the nation had been torn apart by decades of ethnic conflict, political violence, and the collapse of public services. Education and healthcare systems had been gutted. Hundreds of thousands of Ugandans had been killed, displaced, or driven into exile. The Luwero Triangle, the heartland of the NRA's revolution, was a wasteland of mass graves and destroyed villages.

The armed forces were in disarray. The UNLA had disintegrated after the fall of Kampala, but various rebel groups remained active across the country. In the north, former UNLA soldiers and Amin loyalists were regrouping. In the west, the Rwenzururu movement continued to press for autonomy. And on the horizon, a new threat was emerging — the Holy Spirit Movement, led by the self-proclaimed prophet Alice Auma, which would soon become the LRA insurgency that would plague northern Uganda for decades.

Internationally, Uganda was isolated. The country's reputation had been destroyed by Amin's brutality and the chaos of the post-Amin years. Foreign investors had fled, development aid had dried up, and Uganda was widely regarded as a failed state — a cautionary tale of what happens when a nation is governed by dictators.

Into this maelstrom stepped Yoweri Museveni — and he went to work with the same methodical determination that had characterized his guerrilla campaign.

Building the NRM State

The NRM's approach to governance was distinctive in several respects. First, Museveni implemented the "Movement" system — a non-party political framework that, in theory, was designed to transcend ethnic and regional divisions by allowing all Ugandans to participate in politics without the divisive constraints of partisan affiliation. Under this system, all Ugandans were members of the National Resistance Movement by default, and elections were held on a non-partisan basis.

Second, the NRM established the Resistance Councils (RCs) — a system of grassroots governance that drew on the experience of the bush war, when the NRA had established political committees in the areas under its control. The RCs were intended to give ordinary Ugandans a voice in the governance of their communities, from the village level up to the national level. This was, in many respects, an implementation of the grassroots democratic principles that Museveni had studied at Dar es Salaam.

Third, Museveni assembled a government that, while dominated by NRM insiders, included a range of perspectives and talents. The first Prime Minister was Samson Kisekka, a veteran politician and elder statesman. The government included technocrats, former guerrilla commanders, and representatives of various regions and ethnic groups — a deliberate effort to build a broad-based coalition that could command the loyalty of the Ugandan population.

The Challenge of Legitimacy

One of the most significant challenges facing the Museveni government was the question of legitimacy. Having come to power through armed struggle rather than election, the NRM needed to establish its right to govern in the eyes of both the Ugandan people and the international community.

Museveni addressed this challenge in several ways. Domestically, he pointed to the failure of the 1980 elections — which had been widely recognized as rigged — as justification for the armed struggle. He argued that the NRA had fought not to seize power for its own sake but to restore democratic governance that had been stolen by Obote and his UPC party.

Internationally, Museveni positioned Uganda as a champion of human rights, democracy, and economic liberalization — values that resonated with Western governments and international institutions. He invited international observers to monitor his government's activities, cooperated with development agencies, and signaled a willingness to engage with the international community on terms that were mutually beneficial.

But Museveni's most powerful argument was results. Within a few years of taking power, his government had begun to deliver tangible improvements in the lives of ordinary Ugandans: roads were being repaired, schools were reopening, markets were functioning again, and the economy was beginning to grow. In a country where decades of misrule had left millions in abject poverty, these improvements — however modest — were powerful evidence that the NRM government was different from its predecessors.

A Vision for Africa

Even in the earliest days of his presidency, Museveni's vision extended far beyond Uganda's borders. His inaugural address was not merely a statement of domestic policy; it was a statement of principle about the nature of governance in Africa. When he spoke about leaders who wanted to "overstay in power," he was speaking to every African nation that had suffered under dictators who clung to power at the expense of their people.

Museveni understood that Uganda's stability and prosperity were inextricably linked to the stability and prosperity of the broader region. He would spend the coming decades working to build that stability — through military interventions, diplomatic initiatives, economic integration, and the cultivation of relationships with like-minded leaders across the continent.

The foundation was laid on January 26, 1986. The edifice that would be built upon it — for better or worse, depending on one's perspective — would prove to be one of the most consequential in the history of independent Africa.

Chapter Five
Economic Reforms and National Revival
From Ruin to Renaissance

When Yoweri Museveni assumed the presidency in January 1986, Uganda's economy was in a state of catastrophic collapse. GDP had plummeted to less than $4 billion. The country was burdened by massive external debt. Inflation was running at over 200 percent annually. The Ugandan shilling had lost virtually all of its value. Infrastructure — roads, railways, power systems — had been destroyed by decades of conflict. Foreign investment had fled. The formal economy had shrunk to a fraction of its size at independence. Uganda, once one of the most promising economies in East Africa, had become one of the poorest nations on earth.

The transformation that followed — from economic ruin to one of the fastest-growing economies in the world — is one of the most remarkable stories of economic recovery in post-colonial Africa. And at the center of that transformation stood Yoweri Museveni.

The Economic Recovery Programme

The NRM's approach to economic reform was pragmatic rather than ideological. Despite the movement's early socialist leanings — Museveni had studied Marxist theory at Dar es Salaam and initially expressed sympathy for the ideas of Julius Nyerere's Ujamaa — the realities of governing a shattered economy quickly pushed the NRM toward market-oriented reforms.

In 1987, the government launched the Economic Recovery Programme (ERP), a comprehensive package of reforms designed to stabilize the economy, restore confidence, and lay the groundwork for sustained growth. The ERP was developed in close consultation with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank, and it incorporated many of the standard elements of structural adjustment: currency devaluation, reduction of government subsidies, liberalization of trade, privatization of state-owned enterprises, and fiscal discipline.

The currency was devalued to align the official exchange rate with the parallel market rate, a necessary but painful step that restored confidence in the currency and encouraged exports. Government subsidies on basic goods were reduced or eliminated, allowing market forces to set prices. State-owned enterprises that had been run as personal fiefdoms by Amin's cronies were either privatized or closed. Trade barriers were lowered, and Uganda re-engaged with the international trading system.

These reforms were not without pain. In the short term, they led to higher prices for basic goods, increased unemployment in the public sector, and hardship for those who had benefited from the old system of subsidies and state patronage. But in the longer term, they laid the foundation for one of the most impressive economic performances in Africa.

GDP Growth: The Numbers Tell the Story

The results of the NRM's economic reforms were dramatic. In the decade after Museveni took power, Uganda's GDP growth averaged over 6 percent per year — a remarkable achievement for a country that had been in a state of economic freefall. By the 1990s, Uganda was being cited by the World Bank and the IMF as one of the top economic reformers in Africa, a model of what was possible when a government committed itself to sound economic policies.

The numbers tell the story with clarity. Uganda's GDP, which had stood at less than $4 billion when Museveni took power, grew to over $25 billion by the early 2010s and to over $64 billion by 2025. GDP per capita, which had fallen below $200 in the late 1980s, rose to over $1,300 by 2025. Poverty rates, which had exceeded 56 percent in the late 1980s, fell to around 21 percent by 2017 — a reduction of more than half.

Uganda's economic growth was not driven by a single sector but by a broad-based expansion that included agriculture, services, and, increasingly, industry. The country's primary exports — coffee, tea, fish, and flowers — performed well, while new sectors, including telecommunications, financial services, and construction, emerged as engines of growth.

Privatization and the Liberal Economy

The privatization of state-owned enterprises was a central element of the NRM's economic reform programme. Under Amin and Obote, the state had controlled large swathes of the economy, running enterprises in manufacturing, agriculture, mining, and services. Many of these enterprises had been run not for profit but for patronage — serving as vehicles for the distribution of jobs, contracts, and resources to regime loyalists.

The NRM's privatization programme transferred many of these enterprises to the private sector, opening up the economy to competition and investment. The programme was not without its problems — there were allegations of corruption in the privatization process, and some enterprises were sold at below-market prices to politically connected buyers. But the overall effect was to unleash the entrepreneurial energy of the Ugandan people and to create the conditions for private-sector-led growth.

The liberalization of the telecommunications sector was particularly transformative. The introduction of mobile phone networks in the late 1990s and early 2000s — including MTN Uganda, which launched in 1998 — revolutionized business and communication in Uganda. Mobile money, which emerged in the mid-2000s, transformed the financial landscape, bringing millions of previously unbanked Ugandans into the formal financial system.

The Presidential Investors' Conferences

One of the most distinctive features of Museveni's economic management was the Presidential Investors' Conference (PIC) — a regular gathering at which the president personally met with domestic and international investors to discuss business opportunities, regulatory challenges, and investment prospects in Uganda.

The PICs were a signal to the international business community that Uganda was open for business and that the president himself was personally committed to creating a favorable environment for investment. They also served as a mechanism for identifying and addressing specific bottlenecks in the business environment — a practical approach to governance that reflected Museveni's management style.

Under Museveni's leadership, Uganda attracted significant foreign direct investment (FDI), which grew from virtually nothing in the late 1980s to over $10 billion by the mid-2010s. Key sectors for FDI included telecommunications, banking, oil and gas, agriculture, and manufacturing.

The Oil Discovery and Future Prosperity

In 2006, large oil deposits were discovered in the Albertine Graben, a geological formation along the Uganda-Democratic Republic of Congo border. The discovery, estimated at over 6.5 billion barrels of recoverable oil, represented a potential game-changer for the Ugandan economy.

Under Museveni's leadership, the government developed a comprehensive framework for managing oil resources, including the Petroleum Exploration, Production and Development Act, the establishment of the National Oil Company of Uganda, and the development of a sovereign wealth fund. While the management of oil resources has been subject to debate and controversy, the overall framework reflects a commitment to ensuring that Uganda's oil wealth benefits the Ugandan people rather than being captured by a small elite.

The construction of the East African Crude Oil Pipeline (EACOP), which will transport crude oil from Lake Albert to the port of Tanga in Tanzania, represents one of the most significant infrastructure projects in East African history and a testament to Museveni's vision of regional integration and economic cooperation.

The Legacy of Economic Reform

The economic transformation of Uganda under Museveni's leadership is one of the most significant achievements of his presidency. While the country still faces significant challenges — poverty remains widespread, inequality is growing, unemployment among youth is high, and the economy remains vulnerable to external shocks — the foundation that has been laid over the past four decades is substantial.

Uganda's GDP growth has consistently outperformed the African average. The country has attracted billions in foreign investment. A vibrant private sector has emerged, driven by the entrepreneurial energy of the Ugandan people. And the discovery of oil promises to add a new dimension to the economy in the coming decades.

Museveni's economic reforms were not perfect — no reforms are. But they transformed a collapsed economy into one of the most dynamic in East Africa, and they demonstrated that Africa's poverty was not inevitable but was the result of specific policies and choices that could be changed.

"When we sell a kilo of bean coffee in Uganda, we get one dollar per kilo. The same kilo, when it is processed and sold in the UK, goes for $10, $11 or even more a kilo. That is the same situation that goes for all raw materials." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, 2005
Chapter Six
Stability and Security
Bringing Peace to a Broken Land

If there is a single achievement that defines the Museveni presidency, it is the establishment of stability and security in a country that had known neither for over two decades. When Museveni took power in 1986, Uganda was a patchwork of armed groups, rebel militias, and bandit armies, each controlling its own territory and terrorizing the civilian population. The Luwero Triangle was a wasteland of mass graves. The north was destabilized by Amin loyalists and the Karamojong cattle rustlers. The west was home to the Rwenzururu separatist movement. The northeast was ravaged by famine and communal violence.

Under Museveni's leadership, this landscape of chaos was gradually — and sometimes violently — brought under control. The methods were not always pretty, and the costs were sometimes high. But the result was a transformation that allowed millions of Ugandans to live in peace for the first time in their adult lives.

Pacification of the South and West

The first priority of the Museveni government was the pacification of the southern and western regions, which had been the heartland of the NRA during the bush war. These areas were relatively secure by 1986, but pockets of resistance remained — including remnant UNLA forces, bandit groups, and the Rwenzururu movement.

The NRA — which was restructured and renamed the Uganda People's Defence Forces (UPDF) in 1995 — conducted systematic operations to disarm and neutralize these groups. The operations were characterized by the same discipline that had characterized the NRA during the bush war: soldiers were instructed to treat civilians with respect, to avoid unnecessary destruction of property, and to distinguish between combatants and non-combatants.

The pacification of the south and west was relatively swift and successful. By the early 1990s, these regions were largely at peace, and the process of reconstruction and development could begin in earnest.

The Rwenzururu Settlement

One of the most notable early achievements of the Museveni government was the settlement of the Rwenzururu conflict. The Rwenzururu movement, which had been fighting for the autonomy of the Bakonzo and Baamba peoples of the Rwenzori Mountains since the 1960s, was a complex insurgency rooted in ethnic grievances and the marginalization of mountain communities.

Museveni engaged with the Rwenzururu leadership, addressing many of the grievances that had fueled the insurgency. The conflict was gradually de-escalated through a combination of military pressure and political accommodation, and a peace agreement was eventually reached. The Bakonzo and Baamba communities were given greater representation in government, and the Rwenzori region was integrated into the national development framework.

The National Resistance Army and the UPDF

The transformation of the NRA into the UPDF was more than a name change. It was a fundamental reorientation of the armed forces from a revolutionary army to a professional national defense force. Under Museveni's leadership, the UPDF was restructured, retrained, and re-equipped to fulfill its primary mission: the defense of Uganda's territorial integrity and the protection of its citizens.

The UPDF's transformation included the establishment of professional military academies, the introduction of standardized training programs, the development of military doctrine, and the creation of a civilian-military oversight framework. The UPDF also became an active participant in international peacekeeping operations, deploying forces to missions in Somalia, Central African Republic, South Sudan, and other conflict zones.

The UPDF's participation in the African Union Mission in Somalia (AMISOM) was particularly significant. Uganda was the first troop-contributing country to AMISOM, deploying thousands of soldiers to fight al-Shabaab militants in Somalia. The UPDF's performance in Somalia was widely praised, and Uganda's commitment to the mission demonstrated the country's willingness to contribute to regional and international security.

The Regional Security Architecture

Museveni understood that Uganda's security could not be guaranteed in isolation from the security of the broader region. The instability in the Democratic Republic of Congo, the conflicts in South Sudan and the Central African Republic, and the activities of groups like the LRA and al-Shabaab all posed direct threats to Ugandan security.

In response, Museveni played a central role in building the regional security architecture that has helped maintain relative stability in the Great Lakes region. This included the deployment of UPDF forces to Congo (in pursuit of the ADF rebels), South Sudan (to support the government of President Salva Kiir), and the Central African Republic (as part of international peacekeeping efforts).

Uganda also played a key role in the peace processes in South Sudan, Burundi, and the DRC, mediating between warring parties and helping to negotiate ceasefire agreements and power-sharing arrangements. While these efforts have not always been successful, they have helped prevent the escalation of conflicts that could have destabilized the entire region.

The Impact of Stability

The establishment of stability and security in Uganda under Museveni's leadership had profound effects on every aspect of national life. Agriculture, the backbone of the Ugandan economy, could flourish only when farmers could plant and harvest without fear of attack or displacement. Education could function only when schools were safe from rebel raids. Health services could be delivered only when clinics and hospitals were not on the front lines of conflict. Investment could flow only when investors could be confident that their assets would be secure.

The link between stability and development was starkly illustrated by the contrast between the south and west of Uganda — which enjoyed relative peace from the early 1990s onward — and the north, which remained locked in conflict with the LRA until the early 2010s. The south and west experienced rapid economic growth, infrastructure development, and improvements in education and health outcomes, while the north remained one of the poorest and most underdeveloped regions in the country.

This contrast underscored a central truth about development: stability is not a luxury but a prerequisite. Without security, no amount of investment, aid, or good policy can produce sustainable development. Museveni understood this truth in his bones — he had lived it during the bush war — and it informed his approach to governance throughout his presidency.

"We fought a lonely battle against terrorism sponsored by Sudan, and we have defeated it; we cannot be intimidated by any force. For us our guide is the Constitution of Uganda and the laws there under." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, November 26, 2007
Chapter Seven
Education and Health
Building Human Capital for a New Uganda

Of all the achievements of the Museveni presidency, perhaps none has had a more lasting impact on the lives of ordinary Ugandans than the government's investments in education and health. When Museveni took power in 1986, Uganda's education and health systems were in ruins. Schools had been destroyed, teachers had fled, and children had been out of the classroom for years. Hospitals and clinics had been looted, medical supplies had run out, and healthcare workers had abandoned their posts. The HIV/AIDS epidemic was decimating the population, reducing life expectancy to some of the lowest levels in the world.

Under Museveni's leadership, these systems were rebuilt from the ground up, with transformative results.

Universal Primary Education

In January 1997, President Museveni launched the Universal Primary Education (UPE) programme, one of the most ambitious education initiatives in African history. Under UPE, all children in Uganda were guaranteed access to free primary education, with the government paying school fees, providing textbooks, and training teachers.

The results were immediate and dramatic. In the first year of the programme, primary school enrollment surged from approximately 3 million to over 5 million — an increase of nearly 70 percent. By the early 2000s, enrollment had stabilized at around 7 million, and Uganda had achieved near-universal primary education — a milestone that many thought impossible just a decade earlier.

The UPE programme was not without its challenges. The rapid increase in enrollment overwhelmed existing infrastructure, leading to overcrowded classrooms, a shortage of qualified teachers, and a decline in the quality of education. The government responded by investing in the construction of new schools, the training of additional teachers, and the introduction of the Universal Secondary Education (USE) programme in 2005, which extended free education to the secondary level.

Despite these challenges, UPE was a transformative achievement. It gave millions of children — particularly girls and children from rural areas — the opportunity to attend school for the first time. It created a more educated workforce that would drive Uganda's economic growth in the decades ahead. And it demonstrated that Africa's education challenges, while daunting, could be addressed with political will and focused investment.

The Fight Against HIV/AIDS

Uganda's response to the HIV/AIDS epidemic is one of the most celebrated public health success stories in African history. When the epidemic struck Uganda in the early 1980s, the country had one of the highest HIV prevalence rates in the world — estimated at over 15 percent of the adult population by the late 1980s. Life expectancy, which had been rising at independence, plummeted to below 50 years.

Under Museveni's leadership, Uganda launched a comprehensive HIV/AIDS response that combined public education, behavioral change campaigns, condom distribution, and, eventually, antiretroviral therapy (ART). The government's approach was distinctive in its openness and directness — Museveni himself spoke publicly about HIV/AIDS, breaking the taboo that surrounded the disease and encouraging others to do the same.

The results were remarkable. By the mid-2000s, Uganda's HIV prevalence rate had fallen from over 15 percent to approximately 6 percent — one of the most dramatic declines in the world. The reduction was driven by a combination of factors: the government's public education campaigns, which promoted behavioral changes such as condom use and the reduction of multiple sexual partners; the rollout of antiretroviral therapy, which reduced mortality and transmission; and the engagement of communities, religious leaders, and civil society organizations in the response.

Uganda's HIV/AIDS response was widely praised by the international community and became a model for other African countries. The World Health Organization

and UNAIDS recognized Uganda as a model for HIV/AIDS control in resource-limited settings. President Museveni was personally honored for his leadership in the fight against HIV/AIDS, and his approach — combining political leadership, public education, and evidence-based interventions — became a template for other countries.

Uganda's HIV/AIDS response also demonstrated the importance of presidential leadership in public health. Museveni's willingness to speak openly about the disease, to acknowledge the severity of the epidemic, and to commit government resources to the response was critical in breaking the silence and stigma that had hampered the fight against HIV/AIDS in many African countries.

Maternal and Child Health

Under Museveni's leadership, Uganda also made significant progress in maternal and child health. The government invested in the construction and equipping of health centers across the country, trained thousands of midwives and community health workers, and introduced programs to promote prenatal care, immunization, and nutrition.

Infant mortality, which had been one of the highest in the world when Museveni took power, fell by more than 50 percent. Under-five mortality fell similarly. Maternal mortality, while still high by international standards, declined significantly as access to skilled birth attendance and emergency obstetric care improved.

The government also introduced the Village Health Team (VHT) system, which deployed community health workers in every village across the country to provide basic health services, health education, and referral to higher-level health facilities. The VHT system brought primary health care to communities that had never had access to formal health services, and it became a model for community health delivery in other African countries.

Investment in Human Capital

The combined impact of the government's investments in education and health was a dramatic improvement in human capital. Uganda's literacy rate rose from below 50 percent at independence to over 76 percent by the 2010s. Life expectancy, which had fallen below 50 years in the early 1990s due to HIV/AIDS, recovered to over 63 years by the 2020s.

These improvements in human capital were not merely social achievements; they were economic ones. A more educated and healthier workforce was more productive, more innovative, and more capable of driving economic growth. Museveni understood this connection and consistently argued that investments in education and health were not costs but investments — investments that would pay dividends in the form of higher productivity, faster growth, and greater prosperity.

The Museveni government's investments in education and health were not always perfect. The quality of education remained a concern, with many students completing primary school without achieving basic literacy and numeracy. The health system continued to face challenges, including a shortage of healthcare workers, inadequate infrastructure, and uneven access to services. But the overall trajectory was unmistakable: Uganda had made dramatic progress in expanding access to education and health, and millions of Ugandans were better off as a result.

Chapter Eight
Infrastructure
The Backbone of Progress

Infrastructure is the skeleton of a nation. Without roads, bridges, railways, and power systems, no economy can function, no market can operate, and no community can thrive. When Museveni took power in 1986, Uganda's infrastructure was in ruins — roads were cratered and impassable, the railway system had collapsed, power generation was barely sufficient for the capital, and rural areas had no electricity at all. Under his leadership, Uganda embarked on one of the most ambitious infrastructure development programmes in East African history.

Roads and Transport

The rehabilitation and expansion of Uganda's road network was one of the most visible achievements of the Museveni government. From the late 1980s onward, the government invested heavily in the construction and maintenance of roads, connecting communities to markets, schools, and health facilities.

The road network was expanded from approximately 20,000 kilometers in the early 1990s to over 160,000 kilometers by the 2020s, including major highways connecting Kampala to the country's major cities and to neighboring countries. The Entebbe Expressway, completed in 2012, was the first toll highway in Uganda and connected the capital to Entebbe International Airport — a dramatic improvement in the transport infrastructure that supports tourism and international trade.

The construction of the Northern Uganda road corridor, connecting Kampala to the northern cities of Lira, Gulu, and Arua, was particularly significant. This road network, which had been devastated by decades of conflict with the LRA, was rebuilt as part of the government's programme to reintegrate the north into the national economy.

The government also invested in the rehabilitation of the railway system, partnering with the Rift Valley Railways consortium to restore the Uganda Railway, which connects Kampala to the port of Mombasa in Kenya. While the railway rehabilitation has faced challenges, it has improved the movement of goods between Uganda and the coast, reducing transport costs and supporting trade.

Energy: The Karuma and Isimba Dams

Energy is the lifeblood of economic development, and under Museveni's leadership, Uganda undertook a massive expansion of its energy generation capacity. The centerpiece of this expansion was the construction of the Karuma Hydroelectric Power Station, a 600-megawatt dam on the Victoria Nile, which became operational in 2022. Karuma is one of the largest hydroelectric power stations in East Africa and has significantly increased Uganda's energy generation capacity.

The construction of the Isimba Hydroelectric Power Station, a 183-megawatt dam also on the Victoria Nile, complemented Karuma and further expanded the country's energy capacity. Together, these projects have transformed Uganda's energy landscape, moving the country from chronic power shortages to a position of energy surplus that can support industrial development and electrification of rural areas.

The government also invested in the expansion of the electrical grid, extending power lines to rural areas that had never had access to electricity. The Rural Electrification Programme, which began in the early 2000s, connected millions of Ugandans to the national grid, transforming the economic prospects of communities that had previously relied on kerosene lamps and diesel generators.

Telecommunications

The telecommunications revolution that swept Uganda under Museveni's leadership was one of the most transformative developments in the country's modern history. The liberalization of the telecommunications sector in the late 1990s opened the market to private operators, including MTN Uganda, Uganda Telecom, and Airtel Uganda, and the introduction of mobile phone networks transformed business, communication, and social life.

From virtually no mobile phone subscribers in the mid-1990s, Uganda's mobile phone user base grew to over 30 million by the 2020s. Mobile money, introduced in the mid-2000s, transformed the financial landscape, allowing Ugandans to send and receive money, pay bills, and access financial services through their mobile phones. The mobile money revolution was particularly transformative for women and rural communities, who had previously been excluded from the formal financial system.

Entebbe International Airport and Aviation

The expansion and modernization of Entebbe International Airport was another significant infrastructure achievement. The airport, which serves as Uganda's primary international gateway, was expanded and upgraded to handle increased passenger and cargo traffic, including the construction of a new terminal building and the extension of the runway.

The development of regional airports in Gulu, Arua, and Kasese also improved connectivity within Uganda and with neighboring countries, supporting tourism, trade, and regional integration.

The Impact of Infrastructure

The infrastructure development programme under Museveni's leadership had far-reaching effects on the Ugandan economy and society. Improved roads reduced transport costs, connected farmers to markets, and facilitated the movement of goods and people. The expansion of energy generation capacity supported industrial development and attracted investment in manufacturing and processing. The telecommunications revolution connected Ugandans to each other and to the global economy.

These infrastructure investments were not merely physical constructions; they were investments in Uganda's future. They created the foundation for sustained economic growth, improved the quality of life for millions of Ugandans, and positioned Uganda as a competitive economy in the East African region.

Chapter Nine
Foreign Policy and Pan-African Leadership
The Lion on the Regional Stage

Yoweri Museveni is not merely a Ugandan leader; he is an African leader — one whose influence, vision, and actions have shaped the politics and security of the entire East African region and the broader African continent. From the very beginning of his presidency, Museveni understood that Uganda's destiny was inextricably linked to the destiny of its neighbors and the African continent as a whole. His foreign policy, rooted in the Pan-African ideals he had absorbed at the University of Dar es Salaam, has been one of the most active and consequential on the continent.

East African Integration

One of the pillars of Museveni's foreign policy has been the promotion of East African integration. Museveni was one of the driving forces behind the revival of the East African Community (EAC), which was re-established in 2000 after having been dormant since its collapse in 1977. The revived EAC, which initially comprised Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania and later expanded to include Rwanda, Burundi, South Sudan, and the Democratic Republic of Congo, has become one of the most ambitious regional integration projects in Africa.

Under Museveni's leadership, Uganda has been a champion of the EAC's integration agenda, which includes the establishment of a common market, a customs union, and eventually a political federation. Museveni has argued that East African integration is not merely an economic project but a political and security one — that a united East Africa would be better able to resist external pressures, manage regional conflicts, and compete in the global economy.

The EAC's achievements include the elimination of tariff barriers on intra-regional trade, the harmonization of customs procedures, and the development of common infrastructure projects, including the Northern Corridor Transport Improvement Project and the Mombasa-Kampala-Kigali railway project. While the integration process has faced challenges, including disputes over tariff revenues and the slow pace of implementing the common market protocol, it has significantly expanded trade and investment within the region.

The African Union and Pan-Africanism

Museveni has been a consistent advocate for a strong and unified African voice on the world stage. He has supported the African Union's efforts to address the continent's security, economic, and governance challenges, and he has been a vocal critic of what he sees as the paternalism and double standards of Western governments in their dealings with Africa.

Museveni has also been a champion of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), which aims to create a single market for goods and services across the African continent. He has argued that African nations must trade with each other rather than relying on the export of raw materials to the West and the import of finished goods from Asia and Europe.

"Africa is wealthy in natural resources; the problem is they are not optimally utilized." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, 2005

The Great Lakes Region

Museveni's foreign policy has been particularly consequential in the Great Lakes region, where Uganda has played a central role in addressing some of the most complex security challenges on the continent.

During the Rwandan Civil War of 1990-1994, Uganda played a critical role in supporting the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) led by Paul Kagame, which ultimately ended the genocide and established a new government in Kigali. Museveni's decision to support the RPF — and his broader approach to the Great Lakes region — reflected his belief that regional stability could not be achieved through the maintenance of failed states and unjust regimes.

Uganda's involvement in the Congo wars was more controversial. Museveni ordered UPDF forces into the Democratic Republic of Congo in pursuit of rebel groups that were threatening Ugandan security, including the Allied Democratic Forces (ADF) and the Lord's Resistance Army (LRA). While these interventions achieved their immediate military objectives, they also drew criticism from the international community and contributed to the humanitarian crisis in eastern Congo.

Despite these controversies, Museveni's regional engagement has helped prevent the escalation of conflicts that could have destabilized the entire region. Uganda's mediation efforts in South Sudan, Burundi, and the DRC have contributed to the negotiation of peace agreements and the prevention of larger-scale conflicts.

The Non-Aligned Movement and Global South

In January 2024, Museveni was elected as the Chairman of the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM), a position that reflected Uganda's growing influence on the global stage. The NAM, which comprises 120 member states, is one of the largest groupings of countries in the world and serves as a platform for countries that wish to maintain their independence from major power blocs.

Museveni's election as NAM Chairman was a testament to his decades of diplomatic engagement and his reputation as a statesman who has consistently advocated for the interests of the Global South. Under his chairmanship, the NAM has focused on issues including climate change, food security, health, and the reform of international institutions to better reflect the interests and perspectives of developing countries.

Military Diplomacy and Peacekeeping

Uganda's military diplomacy has been a distinctive feature of Museveni's foreign policy. The UPDF has deployed to peacekeeping missions across Africa and beyond, including in Somalia (AMISOM/ATMIS), the Central African Republic, South Sudan, and the Democratic Republic of Congo. These deployments have demonstrated Uganda's willingness to contribute to regional and international security and have enhanced the UPDF's capabilities and reputation.

The UPDF's deployment to Somalia, which began in 2007, was particularly significant. Uganda was the first troop-contributing country to AMISOM, and its soldiers played a central role in the fight against al-Shabaab, including the liberation of Mogadishu and other key towns. The UPDF's performance in Somalia was widely praised, and the deployment demonstrated that African forces could effectively combat terrorism and maintain security in complex environments.

Museveni's foreign policy has been characterized by a combination of pragmatism and principle — a willingness to engage with governments and movements of all ideological stripes in pursuit of Uganda's national interests and the broader goals of African unity and development. While this approach has sometimes been controversial, it has positioned Uganda as a significant player in regional and global affairs.

Chapter Ten
The LRA and Northern Uganda
Defeating the Shadow

Of all the challenges that President Museveni has faced during his four decades in power, none has been more protracted, more painful, or more internationally visible than the insurgency of the Lord's Resistance Army (LRA) in northern Uganda. For nearly two decades, from the late 1980s to the early 2010s, the LRA, led by the self-proclaimed spiritual leader Joseph Kony, terrorized the people of northern Uganda — abducting children to serve as soldiers and sex slaves, mutilating civilians, destroying villages, and creating one of the worst humanitarian crises on the continent.

Museveni's response to the LRA was complex, evolving, and ultimately successful in its primary objective: the defeat of the LRA as a threat to Ugandan security. But the story of the LRA and northern Uganda is also a story of the limits of military power, the difficulties of counterinsurgency warfare, and the challenges of rebuilding communities devastated by decades of conflict.

The Origins of the LRA

The LRA emerged from the complex ethnic and political dynamics of northern Uganda. When Museveni's NRA came to power in 1986, it was perceived by many in the north — particularly the Acholi people, who had dominated the army under Obote and Amin — as a southern and western Bantu force that had conquered the north. The NRA's initial operations in the north, including Operation Simsim, involved heavy-handed military tactics that alienated the civilian population and created the conditions for insurgency.

The Holy Spirit Movement, led by Alice Auma, emerged in 1986-1987 as a spiritual and military resistance to the Museveni government. Auma believed she was possessed by the Holy Spirit and led an army of followers — many of them children — into battle against the NRA. The Holy Spirit Movement was eventually defeated militarily, but its defeat did not end the insurgency. Instead, it gave rise to a more dangerous and enduring movement: the LRA.

Joseph Kony, a former Catholic altar boy from Gulu, had served as a spiritual medium in the Holy Spirit Movement before forming his own group in 1987. The LRA adopted a syncretic blend of Christianity, traditional Acholi spiritualism, and messianic ideology, and it waged a campaign of terror against the civilian population of northern Uganda, often targeting the very communities it claimed to represent.

The Humanitarian Crisis

The LRA insurgency created one of the worst humanitarian crises in Africa. Over the course of nearly two decades, the LRA abducted an estimated 60,000 children, forcing them to serve as soldiers, porters, and sex slaves. The group was responsible for the deaths of an estimated 100,000 people and the displacement of over 2 million others.

The displaced population of northern Uganda — known as the Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) — lived in massive camps that were supposed to provide protection from LRA attacks but often became sites of disease, malnutrition, and further suffering. The IDP camps, some of which housed hundreds of thousands of people, became symbols of the humanitarian catastrophe wrought by the LRA.

Museveni's Response

Museveni's response to the LRA evolved over time. Initially, the government focused on military operations against the LRA, deploying the UPDF to northern Uganda and conducting offensive operations aimed at capturing or killing Kony and his commanders.

These operations achieved mixed results. The LRA's guerrilla tactics — ambushes, night raids, and the abduction of civilians — made it difficult for the UPDF to engage the group in conventional battle. Kony's strategy of using abducted children as fighters and human shields complicated military operations and drew international condemnation. The UPDF's tactics, while effective in some respects, also drew criticism for their impact on civilians.

In 2005, the International Criminal Court (ICC) issued arrest warrants for Kony and several other LRA leaders, charging them with crimes against humanity and war crimes. While the ICC warrants were welcomed by many in northern Uganda, they complicated peace negotiations, as Kony refused to surrender to the ICC.

Museveni pursued both military and diplomatic approaches to the LRA. The government engaged in several rounds of peace negotiations with the LRA, mediated by the government of South Sudan and other regional actors. A comprehensive peace agreement was reached in 2008, but it collapsed when Kony failed to sign it, reportedly because of the ICC arrest warrants.

The Defeat of the LRA

Following the collapse of the peace process, the UPDF launched Operation Lightning Thunder in 2008, a cross-border military operation into the Democratic Republic of Congo aimed at destroying LRA camps and capturing or killing LRA leaders. The operation achieved its immediate military objectives, disrupting LRA operations and forcing the group to scatter into remote areas of the DRC, the Central African Republic, and South Sudan.

In 2011, Museveni authorized the deployment of UPDF forces to the DRC and the Central African Republic to continue pursuing the LRA. These operations, combined with the efforts of the United States African Command (AFRICOM), which deployed special forces to support regional armies, gradually reduced the LRA to a small, fragmented force incapable of mounting large-scale attacks.

By the mid-2010s, the LRA had been effectively defeated as a threat to Ugandan security. While remnants of the group continued to operate in remote areas of the DRC and the Central African Republic, the group's leadership was depleted, its fighting force reduced to a few hundred fighters, and its capacity for large-scale atrocities severely diminished.

Rebuilding Northern Uganda

The defeat of the LRA was only the beginning of the process of rebuilding northern Uganda. The region had been devastated by two decades of conflict, and the challenges of reconstruction were immense: infrastructure had been destroyed, livelihoods had been lost, social structures had been fractured, and a generation of children had been denied education and subjected to horrific trauma.

Under Museveni's leadership, the government launched a comprehensive programme for the reconstruction of northern Uganda, investing in roads, schools, health facilities, and agricultural development. The Peace, Recovery and Development Plan for Northern Uganda (PRDP), launched in 2009, allocated billions of shillings to the reconstruction of the north and the reintegration of displaced communities.

While the reconstruction of northern Uganda has made significant progress, the region continues to face challenges, including high poverty rates, limited economic opportunities, and the long-term effects of trauma and displacement. Museveni has acknowledged these challenges and has continued to prioritize the development of the north in his government's budgetary and policy decisions.

Chapter Eleven
Agriculture and Poverty Eradication
Feeding a Nation, Lifting the Poor

Agriculture is the backbone of the Ugandan economy. Over 70 percent of Uganda's population depends on agriculture for their livelihoods, and the sector accounts for a significant share of the country's GDP and export earnings. Under Museveni's leadership, agriculture has been a central focus of government policy, with the aim of transforming subsistence farming into a commercial enterprise capable of feeding the nation, generating export earnings, and lifting millions out of poverty.

The Agricultural Policy Framework

The Museveni government's approach to agriculture has been guided by a series of policy frameworks and strategies, including the Plan for Modernization of Agriculture (PMA), the National Agricultural Policy, and the Agricultural Sector Strategic Plan. These frameworks have sought to address the key constraints facing Ugandan agriculture: low productivity, limited access to markets, inadequate infrastructure, poor post-harvest handling, and the limited use of improved inputs and technologies.

Key interventions have included the distribution of improved seeds and fertilizers, the construction of rural roads and market infrastructure, the establishment of agricultural cooperatives and marketing associations, the promotion of irrigation, and the introduction of new technologies and farming practices.

Poverty Reduction: The Numbers

Under Museveni's leadership, Uganda achieved one of the most significant poverty reductions in Africa. In 1992, the poverty headcount ratio stood at 56 percent — meaning that more than half of the Ugandan population was living below the national poverty line. By 2017, this figure had fallen to approximately 21 percent — a reduction of more than half in 25 years.

The reduction in poverty was driven by a combination of factors: economic growth, which created employment opportunities; investments in education and health, which improved human capital; infrastructure development, which connected rural communities to markets; and targeted social programmes, which provided support to the most vulnerable.

Museveni's approach to poverty eradication was pragmatic rather than ideological. Rather than relying on wholesale redistribution or state-controlled economic planning, he focused on creating the conditions for private-sector growth and individual enterprise. The philosophy was simple: create the conditions in which people can earn their own way out of poverty, rather than depending on government handouts.

The Parish Development Model

In more recent years, the Museveni government has introduced the Parish Development Model (PDM), a flagship programme aimed at eradicating poverty at the grassroots level. Under the PDM, financial resources are channeled directly to parishes — the lowest administrative unit in Uganda — to support productive activities, including agriculture, livestock keeping, and small-scale enterprise development.

The PDM represents a continuation of Museveni's focus on grassroots development and poverty eradication. By targeting resources at the parish level, the programme aims to reach the poorest and most marginalized communities — those that have been left behind by the broader economic growth of the past four decades.

The Presidential Initiatives

Throughout his presidency, Museveni has championed several presidential initiatives aimed at boosting agricultural production and reducing poverty. These include the Presidential Initiative on Banana Industrial Development, the Presidential Initiative on Roots and Tubers, and the National Coffee Campaign.

Coffee, Uganda's most important export crop, has been a particular focus of Museveni's agricultural strategy. Under his leadership, Uganda has become one of the largest coffee exporters in Africa, and the government has invested in the rehabilitation of coffee-growing areas, the distribution of improved coffee varieties, and the establishment of coffee processing facilities. Museveni has frequently spoken about the importance of value addition in agriculture, arguing that Uganda and Africa in general must move beyond the export of raw materials to the processing and manufacturing of finished goods.

"If we could export more finished products instead of raw materials, we could become a middle-income country." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting, November 2007

The Economic Students' Conferences and Investment Promotion

Museveni's Presidential Investors' Conferences, mentioned in the economic reforms chapter, also had a significant agricultural dimension. By bringing together domestic and international investors with agricultural stakeholders, the conferences helped to attract investment in agro-processing, food manufacturing, and agricultural export ventures.

The result has been the emergence of a more commercialized agricultural sector, with growing investment in modern farming techniques, irrigation, and value addition. While challenges remain — including land fragmentation, climate change, and the limited access of smallholder farmers to credit and markets — the trajectory of Ugandan agriculture under Museveni's leadership has been one of progress and transformation.

The Cooperative Movement and Farmer Organizations

Under Museveni's leadership, the cooperative movement has been revived and strengthened as a tool for empowering smallholder farmers. Agricultural cooperatives enable farmers to pool their resources, access better prices for their products, and negotiate collectively with buyers and processors. The government has supported the formation of cooperatives through training, seed capital, and the establishment of a legal framework that protects the rights and interests of cooperative members.

The coffee cooperative movement has been particularly successful, with organizations like the Uganda Coffee Farmers Alliance and the National Union of Coffee Agro-processors and Development (NUCAD) providing farmers with access to improved varieties, technical assistance, and premium markets. These cooperatives have helped to increase coffee yields and improve the quality of Ugandan coffee, enabling farmers to command higher prices in international markets.

The dairy sector has also benefited from the cooperative movement, with organizations like the Uganda Animal Nutrition and Agricultural Resources (UANAR) and the Lake Mburo Dairy Cooperative providing farmers with access to artificial insemination services, animal feed, and milk collection and processing facilities. Uganda's dairy sector has grown significantly under Museveni's leadership, with milk production increasing from approximately 200 million liters per year in the early 1990s to over 2.5 billion liters by the 2020s.

Irrigation and Climate Resilience

Climate change poses a significant threat to Ugandan agriculture, with rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, and increasing frequency of droughts and floods threatening crop yields and food security. Under Museveni's leadership, the government has invested in irrigation infrastructure and climate resilience measures to help farmers adapt to the changing climate.

The government has invested in the construction and rehabilitation of irrigation schemes across the country, including the Doho Rice Scheme in Tororo, the Mubuku Irrigation Scheme in Kasese, and the Mpologoma Swamp irrigation project. These schemes provide farmers with access to reliable water supplies, enabling them to grow crops year-round and to reduce their dependence on rain-fed agriculture.

The government has also promoted climate-smart agricultural practices, including conservation agriculture, agroforestry, and the use of drought-resistant crop varieties. These practices help farmers to maintain productivity in the face of changing climate conditions while reducing the environmental impact of agricultural activities.

Challenges That Remain

Despite the significant progress in poverty reduction, Uganda continues to face substantial challenges. Youth unemployment remains high. Inequality, as measured by the Gini coefficient, stands at approximately 42.8, indicating significant disparities in income and wealth. Rural poverty remains higher than urban poverty, and some regions — particularly the north and east — continue to lag behind the south and west in development indicators.

Museveni has acknowledged these challenges and has continued to emphasize the importance of sustained economic growth, targeted investment in agriculture, and the creation of employment opportunities for young Ugandans as the keys to continued poverty reduction and the achievement of middle-income status.

Chapter Twelve
Leadership Style
The Museveni Doctrine

What makes a leader effective? Is it charisma? Intelligence? Determination? The ability to inspire? Or is it something more fundamental — a deep understanding of the forces that shape history, combined with the will and the skill to act upon that understanding? Yoweri Museveni's leadership style encompasses all of these qualities, but it is defined above all by a particular combination of intellectual rigor, strategic patience, pragmatic flexibility, and an unwavering commitment to his vision for Uganda and Africa.

The Intellectual Leader

Museveni is, first and foremost, an intellectual leader. He is one of the most well-read heads of state in Africa, with a deep knowledge of history, philosophy, economics, and military strategy. His studies at the University of Dar es Salaam, under the tutelage of Walter Rodney and in the intellectual milieu of Pan-African thought, gave him a theoretical framework for understanding the challenges facing Africa — a framework that has informed his approach to governance throughout his career.

Museveni's intellectualism is not merely academic; it is practical. He has consistently demonstrated the ability to analyze complex situations, identify the key variables, and develop strategies that address the root causes of problems rather than their symptoms. His diagnosis of "the problem of Africa" — leaders who want to stay in power — was not just a sound bite; it was the result of careful analysis of the patterns of governance across the continent.

In economic policy, Museveni has shown a willingness to learn from experience and to adapt his approach based on evidence. While his early instincts were shaped by Marxist and Pan-Africanist thought, he was pragmatic enough to recognize that market-oriented reforms were necessary for economic recovery, and he implemented them with determination and skill.

Strategic Patience

One of the most distinctive features of Museveni's leadership is his strategic patience — his willingness to play the long game, to wait for the right moment to act, and to pursue objectives through indirect means when direct action is not feasible.

This quality was evident during the bush war, when Museveni patiently built the NRA from fewer than 100 fighters to an army of 10,000 over the course of five years, winning the support of the population through political education and disciplined conduct. It was evident in his approach to economic reform, where he understood that transformation would take time and that short-term sacrifices were necessary for long-term gains. And it was evident in his approach to regional security, where he pursued a combination of military pressure and diplomatic engagement over many years to neutralize the LRA threat.

Pragmatic Flexibility

Museveni is not an ideologue. While he has a coherent worldview rooted in Pan-Africanism and developmentalism, he has consistently demonstrated the ability to adapt his approach to changing circumstances. This pragmatic flexibility has been one of the keys to his political longevity and his ability to navigate the complex and often unpredictable world of African politics.

When market-oriented reforms proved necessary for economic recovery, Museveni embraced them despite his early Marxist sympathies. When diplomatic engagement with rebel groups proved more effective than military action, he pursued negotiations. When international partnerships proved beneficial for Uganda's development, he cultivated relationships with governments and institutions of all ideological stripes.

This pragmatism has sometimes been criticized as inconsistency, and there are those who argue that Museveni has been too willing to compromise his principles in pursuit of political objectives. But his supporters argue that pragmatism is not the same as opportunism — that it reflects a mature understanding of the complexity of governance and the need for flexibility in pursuing long-term goals.

Decisive Action

When Museveni determines that action is necessary, he acts decisively and with full commitment. This quality was evident in the bush war, where he committed himself fully to the armed struggle despite overwhelming odds. It was evident in his economic reforms, where he implemented difficult structural adjustment policies despite the political costs. And it was evident in his military interventions, where he deployed UPDF forces to Somalia, Congo, and South Sudan with the determination and resources necessary to achieve their objectives.

Museveni's decisiveness is rooted in his analytical approach: once he has analyzed a situation and determined the appropriate course of action, he pursues it with single-minded determination. This quality has earned him both admiration and criticism — admiration from those who value strong, decisive leadership, and criticism from those who see his decisiveness as a form of authoritarianism.

The People's President

Despite his position at the apex of Ugandan politics for over four decades, Museveni has maintained a remarkably direct connection with ordinary Ugandans. He regularly travels to rural areas, visits markets and farms, and engages in direct conversations with citizens about their concerns and aspirations. His public addresses are characterized by their directness and accessibility — he speaks in plain language, avoids bureaucratic jargon, and addresses the issues that matter most to ordinary people.

This connection with the people has been one of the keys to Museveni's political longevity. While his opponents have accused him of manipulation and electoral fraud, his supporters point to his genuine engagement with the concerns of ordinary Ugandans and his track record of delivering improvements in their lives as evidence of his legitimacy.

"Some people think that being in government for a long time is a bad thing. But the more you stay, the more you learn. I am now an expert in governance." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, 2012

The Manager President

Museveni is often described as a "manager president" — a leader who approaches governance with the methodical precision of a project manager. He is known for his detailed knowledge of government budgets, his insistence on monitoring and evaluation of government programmes, and his practice of personally following up on the implementation of key projects.

This management-oriented approach is evident in the way Museveni conducts his public business. He is known to chair marathon cabinet meetings that can last for eight or more hours, during which he quizzes ministers on the details of their portfolios, reviews budget performance, and makes decisions on the spot. He is also known for his practice of making field visits to government projects — schools, roads, health centers, and construction sites — where he personally inspects progress and holds project managers accountable for delays and cost overruns.

This hands-on approach has earned Museveni a reputation as a detail-oriented leader who demands results from his government. While some critics argue that this micro-management style can stifle initiative and create bottlenecks, his supporters point to the tangible results — the roads that were built, the schools that were opened, the health centers that were equipped — as evidence that his approach works.

The Role of the First Lady

No account of Museveni's leadership style would be complete without mention of the role played by Janet Museveni, his wife of over fifty years. Janet Museveni has been a key figure in the Museveni presidency, serving in several ministerial capacities and playing a significant role in the government's social development programmes.

As Minister of Education and Sports, Janet Museveni oversaw the expansion of the Universal Secondary Education programme and the development of Uganda's sports infrastructure. Her tenure was marked by a focus on girl-child education, teacher training, and the improvement of school facilities. She has also been active in advocacy for maternal health, HIV/AIDS prevention, and the empowerment of women and girls.

The partnership between Yoweri and Janet Museveni has been a source of stability and continuity in the Museveni presidency. Their shared commitment to public service and their complementary roles in government have strengthened the NRM's governance and broadened its appeal to women and families across Uganda.

The Museveni Doctrine: Lessons for Africa

Museveni's leadership style, distilled to its essence, can be understood as a set of principles that have guided his governance for over four decades. These principles — intellectual rigor, strategic patience, pragmatic flexibility, decisive action, and connection to the people — constitute what might be called the "Museveni Doctrine" of African leadership.

The Museveni Doctrine holds that African leaders must be intellectually prepared for the challenges of governance — that they must understand the historical, economic, and political forces that shape the continent, and that they must develop the analytical skills necessary to navigate these forces effectively. It holds that good governance requires patience — that transformation does not happen overnight and that leaders must be willing to invest in long-term strategies even when short-term results are elusive. It holds that pragmatism is not opportunism but wisdom — that leaders must be willing to adapt their approach to changing circumstances while remaining faithful to their core principles. And it holds that the ultimate test of leadership is results — that leaders must be judged not by their intentions but by their achievements.

A Legacy of Leadership

Museveni's leadership style — intellectual, patient, pragmatic, decisive, and connected to the people — has defined the character of the Ugandan state for over four decades. Whether one admires or criticizes his leadership, there is no denying its impact. Museveni has shaped Uganda in ways that will be felt for generations, and his approach to leadership will continue to be studied and debated long after he leaves the stage.

Chapter Thirteen
Challenges and the Art of Navigation
Weathering the Storms

No leader governs without challenges, and Yoweri Museveni has faced some of the most complex and demanding challenges of any leader on the African continent. From the early days of his presidency, when he confronted the task of rebuilding a shattered state, to the more recent challenges of managing a growing economy, navigating regional conflicts, and responding to the demands of a young and increasingly connected population, Museveni has demonstrated a remarkable ability to weather storms that would have overwhelmed many leaders.

The Constitutional Question

Perhaps the most significant political challenge facing Museveni has been the question of his continued hold on power. In his 1986 inaugural address, Museveni had identified "leaders who want to overstay in power" as the fundamental problem of African governance. Yet, as his presidency extended beyond two decades, the question of term limits became increasingly contentious.

In 2005, a constitutional referendum abolished presidential term limits, allowing Museveni to run for a third term. The decision was controversial, both domestically and internationally, and it drew criticism from opposition groups, civil society organizations, and some Western governments. Museveni defended the decision by arguing that the will of the people — as expressed through the parliamentary process and the referendum — should be respected, and that removing term limits was not the same as removing democracy.

In 2017, a further constitutional amendment removed the presidential age limit, which had previously prevented anyone over 75 from running for president. This amendment was also controversial, and it drew significant opposition from civil society and the political opposition.

Museveni has defended his continued presidency by pointing to his track record of delivering stability, economic growth, and social development. He has argued that his experience and expertise are valuable assets that should not be discarded prematurely, and that the Ugandan people have the right to choose their leader through democratic elections.

Human Rights Concerns

Museveni's presidency has faced persistent criticism from human rights organizations regarding the treatment of political opponents, restrictions on press freedom, and the use of security forces to suppress protests. International organizations, including Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International, have documented instances of arbitrary detention, extrajudicial killings, and the excessive use of force by security forces.

The Museveni government has responded to these criticisms by pointing to the improvements in human rights that have occurred under its leadership — including the restoration of the rule of law, the protection of property rights, and the expansion of civil liberties — compared to the previous regimes of Amin and Obote. The government has also argued that some of the measures criticized by human rights organizations are necessary responses to genuine security threats, including terrorism and organized crime.

Corruption

Corruption has been a persistent challenge for the Museveni government. Despite the government's efforts to combat corruption through institutions such as the Inspectorate of Government and the Anti-Corruption Court, corruption remains widespread in the public sector, from petty bribery to large-scale procurement fraud.

The most notable corruption scandal during Museveni's presidency was the embezzlement of $12.6 million in donor funds from the Office of the Prime Minister in 2012. The funds had been earmarked for the reconstruction of northern Uganda and the development of the Karamoja region, and their embezzlement prompted the suspension of aid by the European Union, the United Kingdom, Germany, Denmark, Ireland, and Norway.

Museveni has acknowledged the problem of corruption and has taken steps to address it, including the prosecution of senior officials and the strengthening of anti-corruption institutions. However, critics argue that corruption remains deeply embedded in the political system and that the government's efforts to combat it have been insufficient.

Youth Unemployment

Uganda's young and rapidly growing population presents both an opportunity and a challenge. With over 75 percent of the population under the age of 30, Uganda has one of the youngest populations in the world — a demographic dividend that could fuel decades of economic growth if properly harnessed.

However, the rapid growth of the labor force has outpaced the creation of formal employment opportunities, leading to high rates of youth unemployment. The government has responded with programs such as the Youth Livelihood Programme and the Emyooga initiative, which provide young Ugandans with skills training, startup capital, and mentoring. While these programs have reached millions of young people, the challenge of youth unemployment remains significant.

Political Opposition

Museveni has faced increasingly vocal political opposition, particularly in the form of the National Unity Platform (NUP), led by musician-turned-politician Bobi Wine (Robert Kyagulanyi). Bobi Wine's popularity among young, urban Ugandans has made him a formidable challenger, and his campaigns have drawn large crowds and significant international attention.

The government's response to the opposition has been a source of controversy, with critics alleging the use of excessive force, arbitrary detention, and electoral manipulation. Museveni and his supporters argue that the government has respected the democratic process, pointing to the regular holding of elections and the peaceful transfer of power within the NRM.

Navigating the Challenges

What distinguishes Museveni from many other long-serving leaders is his ability to navigate these challenges while maintaining the stability and economic growth that have been the hallmarks of his presidency. He has shown a willingness to adapt his approach in response to changing circumstances — introducing multi-party politics in 2005, implementing economic reforms in response to changing global conditions, and adjusting his security strategy in response to new threats.

Whether this adaptability reflects genuine statesmanship or political calculation is a matter of perspective. But the results are difficult to deny: Uganda has experienced over four decades of relative stability, sustained economic growth, and significant improvements in human development indicators under Museveni's leadership.

The challenges are real, and they are significant. But Museveni has faced greater challenges before — the bush war, the LRA insurgency, the HIV/AIDS epidemic — and he has emerged from each one with his position strengthened and his country better off than it was before.

Chapter Fifteen
Legacy
The Lion of the Pearl

Every great leader leaves a legacy — a set of achievements, decisions, and consequences that define their impact on history. For Yoweri Kaguta Museveni, the legacy is complex, multifaceted, and consequential. After more than four decades at the helm of Ugandan politics — from the revolution that brought him to power in 1986 to the present day — Museveni has left an indelible mark on Uganda, on East Africa, and on the African continent as a whole.

The Legacy of Stability

The most fundamental legacy of the Museveni presidency is stability. When Museveni took power in 1986, Uganda was a shattered state — torn apart by decades of dictatorship, civil war, and economic collapse. The economy had collapsed, infrastructure lay in ruins, and hundreds of thousands of Ugandans had been killed. The country was widely regarded as a failed state.

Under Museveni's leadership, this landscape of chaos was transformed. The civil wars were brought to an end. The economy was rebuilt. Infrastructure was constructed. Institutions were established. And, for the first time in decades, millions of Ugandans were able to live in peace, raise their families, educate their children, and build their livelihoods without the constant threat of violence.

This legacy of stability cannot be overstated. In a region that has been plagued by conflict — from the Rwandan genocide to the Congo wars to the South Sudan civil war — Uganda under Museveni has been an island of relative stability, a country where the rule of law has been maintained, where markets have functioned, and where ordinary people have been able to go about their daily lives without fear.

The Legacy of Economic Transformation

Museveni's economic legacy is one of transformation. From an economy that had collapsed to less than $4 billion in GDP, Uganda has grown to over $64 billion in GDP, making it one of the largest economies in East Africa. The country has consistently ranked among the fastest-growing economies in the world, and it has attracted billions of dollars in foreign investment.

The discovery of oil in the Albertine Graben — estimated at over 6.5 billion barrels of recoverable reserves — promises to add a new dimension to the economy and to position Uganda as a significant player in the global energy market. The development of the oil sector, including the construction of the East African Crude Oil Pipeline, represents one of the most significant economic developments in Uganda's history and a testament to Museveni's vision of using natural resources to drive national development.

The emergence of a vibrant private sector, the expansion of the telecommunications industry, the growth of the financial services sector, and the modernization of agriculture are all part of the economic legacy of the Museveni era. While challenges remain — including poverty, inequality, and youth unemployment — the economic foundation that has been laid is substantial and will continue to generate growth and opportunity for generations to come.

The Legacy of Human Development

The improvements in education and health under Museveni's leadership have transformed the lives of millions of Ugandans. Universal Primary Education gave millions of children the opportunity to attend school for the first time. The fight against HIV/AIDS saved hundreds of thousands of lives and made Uganda a model for public health on the continent. Investments in maternal and child health have reduced mortality rates and improved the quality of life for women and children.

The increase in literacy from below 50 percent at independence to over 76 percent by the 2010s, the recovery of life expectancy from below 50 years in the early 1990s to over 63 years by the 2020s, and the reduction in poverty from 56 percent to 21 percent are among the most significant achievements of the Museveni era. These are not just statistics; they are the lives of real people — children who went to school for the first time, parents who were able to access healthcare, families who were lifted out of poverty.

The Legacy of Regional Leadership

Museveni's legacy extends beyond Uganda's borders. His role in the revival of the East African Community, his leadership in the fight against al-Shabaab in Somalia, his mediation of regional conflicts, and his advocacy for Pan-African integration have positioned Uganda as a significant player in regional and continental affairs.

Under Museveni's leadership, Uganda has become one of the largest troop-contributing countries to African Union peacekeeping operations, deploying forces to Somalia, the Central African Republic, and South Sudan. The UPDF's performance in these missions has been widely praised and has demonstrated Uganda's commitment to regional security.

Museveni's election as Chairman of the Non-Aligned Movement in January 2024 was a recognition of his stature as one of Africa's most experienced and influential leaders. The position gave him a platform to advocate for the interests of the Global South and to shape the agenda of an organization comprising 120 member states.

The Legacy of Ideas

Perhaps the most enduring legacy of the Museveni era is the legacy of ideas. His diagnosis of "the problem of Africa" — leaders who want to stay in power — has become one of the most widely quoted and debated observations in African political thought. His approach to governance — combining grassroots democracy, pragmatic economic reform, and Pan-African idealism — has influenced political movements and governments across the continent.

Museveni's intellectual contributions — his writings, speeches, and public addresses — have enriched the discourse on African governance and development. His analysis of the structural forces that keep Africa poor — exploitative trade relationships, the export of raw materials, the lack of industrialization — has resonated with leaders and thinkers across the continent and beyond.

The Weight of History

Every legacy is imperfect, and Museveni's is no exception. The concerns about political freedoms, the questions about term limits, the allegations of corruption, and the challenges of youth unemployment are all part of the complex tapestry of the Museveni era. These are issues that will continue to be debated and analyzed long after the present generation has passed.

But when the full history of the Museveni era is written — when the assessments have been made and the debates have been settled — the fundamental achievement will be clear: Yoweri Kaguta Museveni took a broken country and rebuilt it. He took a shattered economy and made it grow. He took a population ravaged by disease and gave them health. He took a generation denied education and gave them knowledge. He took a region wracked by conflict and helped to build peace.

Uganda in the 2020s: The Fruits of Four Decades

The Uganda of the 2020s is a fundamentally different country from the Uganda that Museveni inherited in 1986. The GDP has grown from less than $4 billion to over $64 billion. The population has grown from approximately 14 million to over 45 million. Life expectancy has risen from below 50 years to over 63 years. Literacy has increased from below 50 percent to over 76 percent. Poverty rates have fallen from over 56 percent to approximately 21 percent. These are not abstract statistics; they represent the lived experiences of millions of Ugandans whose lives have been transformed by the changes of the past four decades.

The Kampala of the 2020s is a bustling, modern city — a far cry from the war-ravaged capital that Museveni captured in January 1986. The skyline is filled with new buildings, shopping malls, and hotels. The streets are lined with mobile phone shops, banks, and businesses. The markets are vibrant with commerce. And the people — young, energetic, and increasingly connected to the global economy — are the driving force behind a dynamic and growing economy.

But the Uganda of the 2020s is also a country of contrasts. The south and west are relatively prosperous, with growing towns, modern infrastructure, and expanding economic opportunities. The north and east, still recovering from decades of conflict and neglect, lag behind in development indicators. The urban areas are connected to the global economy through mobile phones, the internet, and international trade. The rural areas, where the majority of the population still lives, are characterized by subsistence agriculture, limited access to services, and the slow pace of modernization.

These contrasts are not unique to Uganda; they are characteristic of developing countries across Africa and the world. But they are a reminder that the journey of development is long and that the achievements of the past four decades, however significant, are only the beginning of a much longer process.

The Museveni Generation

One of the most significant but often overlooked aspects of Museveni's legacy is the impact he has had on an entire generation of Ugandans. The generation that grew up under Museveni's presidency — the "Museveni generation" — has experienced a fundamentally different Uganda from the one their parents knew. They have grown up in relative peace, attended school through UPE, used mobile phones and mobile money, and benefited from the economic opportunities created by decades of growth.

This generation is more educated, more connected, and more globally aware than any previous generation of Ugandans. They are the entrepreneurs, the technologists, the professionals, and the leaders who will shape Uganda's future. And while they may have different political views and aspirations from those of the Museveni era, they are the living legacy of the investments in education, health, and economic development that have characterized the past four decades.

A Legacy for the Ages

That is the legacy of the Lion of the Pearl.

"The more you stay, the more you learn. I am now an expert in governance." — Yoweri Kaguta Museveni

As Uganda looks to the future — to the promise of oil, to the potential of its young population, to the possibilities of regional integration and continental cooperation — the legacy of Museveni will serve as both a foundation and a reference point. The achievements are real, the challenges are significant, and the story is still being written.

But one thing is certain: Yoweri Kaguta Museveni has shaped Uganda, and in doing so, has shaped the narrative of modern Africa. His story — from the hills of Ntungamo to the halls of power, from the forests of the Luwero Triangle to the conference rooms of the African Union — is a story of determination, vision, and impact on a scale that few leaders in African history can match.

The Final Verdict

History, they say, is written by the victors. But history is also written by time — and time has a way of revealing the truth about leaders and their legacies. When the full measure of Yoweri Museveni's presidency is taken — not in the heat of political debate but in the calm light of historical reflection — the verdict will likely be that he was one of the most consequential leaders in the history of independent Africa.

He was a leader who took power in a moment of crisis and used that crisis as an opportunity for transformation. He was a leader who understood that the liberation of a people is not a single act but a continuous process — a process that requires constant effort, constant adaptation, and constant commitment to the well-being of the nation. He was a leader who, for all his imperfections, delivered tangible improvements in the lives of millions of Ugandans and helped to establish Uganda as a stable, growing, and increasingly influential nation in East Africa and beyond.

The Museveni era will be studied and debated for generations to come. Scholars will analyze his economic reforms, his security policies, his foreign engagements, and his approach to governance. Politicians will draw lessons from his successes and his mistakes. And the Ugandan people — the ultimate judges of any leader — will continue to weigh his achievements against his shortcomings and decide, as they have done in election after election, whether his leadership has served their interests.

What is beyond debate is the scale and significance of his impact. Yoweri Kaguta Museveni did not merely serve as president of Uganda; he transformed Uganda. He did not merely govern a nation; he built a nation. And in doing so, he wrote himself into the history of Africa as one of its most enduring, decisive, and consequential leaders.

This biography has sought to capture that story — to tell it fully, honestly, and with the respect it deserves. Whether you are a Ugandan seeking to understand your own history, an African seeking to learn from the experiences of a fellow nation, or a student of leadership seeking to understand what it takes to transform a country, we hope that this account has provided insight, understanding, and perhaps even inspiration.

The story of Yoweri Kaguta Museveni is, in the end, the story of Uganda itself — a story of struggle, of transformation, and of the enduring power of vision and determination to change the course of history.

May this biography serve as a testament to the power of leadership, the resilience of a people, and the promise of Africa's future. For in the story of Museveni, we see not just the story of one man or one nation, but the story of a continent rising.

## Epilogue: The Long Sunset The first light of January 2026 broke over Kampala as it always does — reluctantly, a slow bleed of orange through the haze that gathers above the city. By mid-morning, the electoral commission had made it official: Yoweri Kaguta Museveni had won a sixth elected term, extending his presidency into a fifth decade. The margin — roughly sixty-one percent against his nearest challenger — was enough to end the contest on the first ballot, though not enough to silence the accusations of rigging that had become as predictable as the results themselves. In Ntungamo, his ancestral home, his supporters danced in the red-earth streets. In Kamwokya and other opposition strongholds, soldiers in camouflage stood guard over polling stations where the count had been disputed. And at State House, Entebbe, the President of the Republic — now in his eighty-second year — prepared to address a nation that had known no other leader for most of its citizens' lives. It had been forty-five years since the young guerrilla commander had ridden into Kampala at the head of the NRA. The man who appeared on screens that evening was recognizably the same figure — the broad face, the distinctive cadence of his speech, the tendency to drift from the prepared text into long, meandering explanations of the economy or the colonial history of the Great Lakes region. But there was a new gravity in his bearing. The war of the 1980s had been fought with a clarity of purpose; the governance of the 2020s demanded something different — patience, negotiation, the management of a country whose ambitions had begun to outgrow its founding generation. The 2021 election had been the most contested of his long reign. The challenge had come not from the old political class but from a new phenomenon: Robert Kyagulanyi Ssentamu, the musician known as Bobi Wine, who had transformed youthful frustration into a political movement in half a decade. Museveni had won that contest with fifty-nine percent of the vote, but the battle had been uglier than the outcome suggested. The lockdown during the COVID-19 pandemic had given security forces latitude that international observers found troubling. The opposition leader had been arrested repeatedly, his rallies dispersed, his supporters killed in clashes that the state characterized as unfortunate but provoked. The Supreme Court upheld the result, but the legitimacy of the process was questioned in the streets of Kampala and in the chanceries of Washington and London. For the first time, the international community that had so long indulged Museveni as a stabilizer began to voice its doubts with an edge of impatience. Museveni's response was characteristic. He did not retreat into defensiveness; he launched a counter-offensive of ideas. The West, he argued, had no standing to lecture him on democracy when it had supported the monarchies of the Gulf and ignored the autocracies of Asia. He pointed to what he insisted were the real indicators of his mandate: an economy that had recovered from the pandemic with growth above six percent, a vaccination campaign that had reached the remotest villages of Karamoja, and the fact that the opposition, for all its energy, remained confined to the urban south and the Instagram feeds of the middle class. "The people who vote are not the people who tweet," he said at a press conference in 2022, a phrase that was derided by critics and quoted approvingly by his supporters — and which, whatever one thought of it, captured a fundamental truth of Ugandan politics. The pandemic years had tested the country in ways that the bush war had not. Uganda closed its borders early, imposed one of the most stringent lockdowns in Africa, and suffered economically as a result. But by the end of 2021, the economy had rebounded with a vigour that surprised even the World Bank. The oil sector — long a promise, now a project — began to move toward reality. In 2022, the government signed off on the East African Crude Oil Pipeline, a 1,443-kilometre artery that would carry Uganda's oil to the Tanzanian coast at Tanga. Environmentalists warned of ecological catastrophe; the global community, increasingly allergic to fossil fuel investments, pressed Uganda to slow down. Museveni was unmoved. "Africa has been told to remain poor for the sake of the climate," he told an audience of petroleum investors in Kampala. "We will develop our resources, and we will do so responsibly." By 2023, the first oil had not yet flowed, but the infrastructure of extraction — the rigs in the Albertine Graben, the industrial parks in Hoima, the refinery plans at Kabaale — had transformed the economy of the west. Contractors from China, Italy, and France moved through the region in fleets of white SUVs. Local businesses boomed. Land prices in the oil corridor multiplied. And the central government, deriving new revenue streams, began to spend on infrastructure with a new intensity: roads that had been planned for a decade were suddenly accelerated, a standard-gauge railway was revived, and the dream of a new international airport at Kabaale took concrete shape. The promise of oil had been the subtext of Museveni's later presidency; by the mid-2020s, it had become the text. The regional stage meanwhile had grown more dangerous, and more demanding. Uganda had become the indispensable power of the Great Lakes — a role that Museveni, with his deep history in the region's wars, seemed uniquely qualified to play. The Ugandan People's Defence Forces had been operating in the Democratic Republic of Congo since 2021, in coordination with Kinshasa, to hunt the ADF, an Islamist group with roots in the old insurgencies of the 1990s. The operations were controversial — critics noted that Uganda's soldiers had been extracting minerals from eastern Congo for three decades — but the official rationale was unimpeachable: these were terrorists who had bombed schools and beheaded civilians in Uganda, and they had to be stopped. In 2024, the UPDF reported that it had killed hundreds of ADF fighters and destroyed their bases. The group remained resilient, a hydra that regrew its heads, but it was no longer the existential threat it had once been. In South Sudan, Museveni continued his long, patient mediation between the factions of that fractured nation, hosting negotiations that were as much about protecting Uganda's interests as about bringing peace to its northern neighbour. In Somalia, Ugandan troops remained the backbone of the African Union mission, holding the line against al-Shabaab for more than a decade and a half. And in the increasingly volatile relationship between the Great Lakes states — the tensions between Rwanda and the DRC, the periodic flare-ups on the Rwenzori border, the diplomatic dance with Burundi — Uganda under Museveni had become the anchor, the country that could speak to all sides because it knew all sides personally. The question that hung over everything, however, was the question of succession. No issue so dominated the late years of the Museveni era as the problem of what came after. It was a question asked in every boardroom and every bar, in every foreign embassy and every local council meeting. And it was a question that the President himself refused to answer directly. "The issue is not who leads, but what the country needs," he said repeatedly, deflecting the enquiry with a smile that conceded nothing. But the country knew that the issue was not abstract. It was embodied in the ambitions of his son, General Muhoozi Kainerugaba, whose social media presence and political interventions had made him the most discussed figure in Ugandan politics after his father. The drama had come to a head in 2022, when Muhoozi, then a senior officer commanding the land forces, used Twitter to discuss invading neighbouring Kenya and to dismiss entire ethnic groups in terms that forced his father to act. The general was removed from his command, publicly rebuked, and the country held its breath. But the removal was seen as calibrated rather than decisive — a tactical withdrawal, not a final rejection. Muhoozi remained in the army, remained close to his father, and remained, in the minds of most political observers, the most probable heir to the dynasty. The NRM defended this by pointing to the family's law — no provision prevented a Kainerugaba from holding high office in the future. The opposition saw in it the shadow of monarchy. And the public, broadly, seemed resigned: if Museveni's son was not the answer, no one else was offering a better one. Against this backdrop, the 2026 election campaign had a curious quality of the preordained. Bobi Wine and his National Unity Platform ran a campaign of furious energy but limited reach, once again blocked from many parts of the country by police and military roadblocks. Museveni ran as he always ran — not as a candidate but as an incumbent, opening roads and bridges and factories, presiding over regional summits, and carrying his famous walking stick into districts where he was greeted as a father returning home. The international observers who arrived for the vote produced qualified reports — yes, the process was well-organized, yes, the margins were wide enough that significant manipulation would have been both difficult and unnecessary, but no, the playing field had not been level. Museveni's victory was announced to a muted Kampala. The streets did not erupt in celebration; they also, notably, did not burn. The address that January evening was perhaps the most reflective of his career. He did not begin with the usual assertion of electoral authority. Instead, he began with memory — with the hills of Kikagati, with his uncle's cattle, with the long walk to Mbarara and the night bus to Dar es Salaam. He spoke of the NRA's early casualty lists, of the comrades who had died in the Luwero Triangle and whose graves were now commemorated at the national shrine in Wobulenzi. He spoke of the poverty he had found on taking power — the empty shelves, the broken roads, the national coffers holding less than a billion shillings. And then he turned to the present. "We have built," he said, "not without mistakes, not without stumbling, but we have built. When I first came to Kampala, this city was a town of refugees. Now it is a capital of the continent. When I first took the oath of office, our children died of measles and malaria in their hundreds of thousands. Now they go to school. That is what I want you to remember." The speech was long, as his speeches always are, and it drifted, as his speeches always do. But it had a valedictory cast that was unmistakable. The President of Uganda had begun to sum up. Not to announce his departure — the constitution he had amended in 2017 allowed him to stand again indefinitely, and there was no indication, despite persistent rumours, that he intended to step aside before health or fate compelled him. But there was a shift in tone, a recognition of season. The rains were ending. The long sun was low. What is one to make of this man, at the end of his political autumn? The record can be read in many ways, each containing its portion of truth. There is the record of the soldier — the guerrilla leader who won a war, ended the tyranny of two dictators, and brought to the Great Lakes a stability that, however imperfect, was real. There is the record of the modernizer — the reformer who opened the economy, rebuilt the state, and led Uganda through its longest period of sustained growth since independence. And there is the record of the father-president — the ruler who, like so many of Africa's founders, could not quite bring himself to trust the collective leadership he insisted he was building, who found in his son's loyalty a comfort that the institutions he had founded could never provide, and who by the end embodied both the promise and the paradox of the liberation movement he had led. He is not, as the final verdicts must acknowledge, an easy figure to love. The authoritarian reflexes are real; the tolerance for dissent is thin; the capacity for self-justification is legendary. And yet the achievements stand. Uganda in 2026 is a country at peace with its neighbours, a country with a growing economy and a young, hopeful population, a country that has experienced five decades without the collapse that so many of its contemporaries suffered. Some of that is luck — geography, the absence of ethnic fault lines that elsewhere proved catastrophic. Much of it is leadership, exercised with a singular consistency of purpose. The night after the election, Museveni stood on the balcony of State House, a building he had entered on a wave of revolutionary hope in 1986. Below him, not the crowds of 1986 — those were smaller, armed, expectant — but a scattering of the faithful, singing the old songs. He waved, and the years seemed to compress. For a moment, in the flickering light, he was the young man in the green beret once more. Then he turned and walked back inside, and the door closed, and the country settled into the long night of its future — a future that would be written, for good or ill, in the shadow of everything he had built. The story, as he would be the first to remind you, is not finished. The oil fields are still being developed. The young population is still restive. The continent is still rising. And the old lion, though slowed, is not yet gone. He has outlasted the colonial order that shaped him, the liberation movements that forged him, and most of the international figures who once dismissed him. He has survived coups, rebellions, pandemics, and elections. He has built a nation in the image of his own long patience. Whether that image will endure beyond him is the question that history will answer. In the meantime, there is only the record, and the man, and the remarkable country they have made together. ## The Heir Question The morning after the election found Kampala in a mood that was difficult to name. The city had gone to bed with the usual suspicions and awakened to the usual certainties. The results were announced, certified, and absorbed. There would be no revolution, no dramatic stand, no convulsion. There was, instead, the quiet acceptance of a people who had learned, across five decades, exactly what to expect from their politics. In the markets and the taxi parks, in the offices and the churches, the conversation turned almost immediately to the next question — the one that no one could answer, but everyone felt obliged to discuss. What came after the old man? It was a question that had haunted the Museveni era from its middle years onward, and it had acquired a particular texture in the new term. The opposition, exhausted and outmanoeuvred, had settled into a rhythm of ritual protest and ritual suppression. The international community, distracted by wars on three continents, had reduced its engagement with Uganda to routine statements and periodic scoldings. The economy, growing but unequal, produced enough to sustain hope and not enough to extinguish grievance. And at the centre of it all sat the President, an octogenarian with the energy of a man half his age, still rising before dawn, still reading his intelligence briefings, still summoning ministers for lectures that could last four hours. He had not slowed, as everyone had predicted he would. But he had begun to measure, to consolidate, to arrange his affairs. The most visible expression of this arranging was the careful management of his son. General Muhoozi Kainerugaba had spent the years since the 2022 Twitter incident in a peculiar limbo — publicly disciplined, privately promoted, and increasingly present at his father's side. In April of 2023 he had been elevated to the rank of full general, a position that made him the highest-ranking officer in the Uganda People's Defence Forces after the Chief of Defence Forces himself. The promotion had been announced with the usual official obliqueness — "recognizing his distinguished service" — but the country understood it as a message. The general was being prepared for something. Whether that something was the presidency or the management of the presidency, the guard was being changed, and the public watched with a mixture of resignation and unease. Muhoozi did little to quiet the speculation. His social media presence continued to be a rolling emergency in miniature — a strange mix of the messianic and the pugilistic, with poems about his father, threats directed at critics, and occasional professions of Christian faith interspersed with cricket scores. He spoke about "the project" of Uganda's liberation in terms that echoed his father's early speeches, and he gathered around himself a coterie of officers and advisors whose loyalty to him was more fervent than their loyalty to the institutions they served. The army, which had once been the NRM's great equalizer — a force drawn from every region, every ethnic group, every class — had become visibly a family affair at its upper echelons. The senior positions were held by men who had served under the elder Museveni; the generation below them was dominated by the general's contemporaries; and the generation below that, young officers who had known no other commander-in-chief, tended to look to Muhoozi as the natural custodian of the revolution's future. The President was aware of this, of course. He had always been aware of everything. But he had chosen — deliberately, it seemed — to address it not by naming a successor but by managing the conditions under which a succession would occur. He had amended the constitution to remove the age limit, so that no legal barrier would force him out. He had maintained the practice, established in 1986, of the NRM choosing its candidate through a delegates' conference — a process that, in practice, functioned as a ratification of the incumbent's preference. And he had spoken, in his rambling way, about the dangers of leadership vacuum. "A river that has been flowing for fifty years does not simply disappear," he said in one of his post-election addresses. "It must be directed. It must be channelled. Or it will flood the land." The metaphor was vintage Museveni — agrarian, patient, and utterly non-specific. But it was read, in the salons of Kampala and the chanceries of the world, as an admission that the succession would not be a moment but a process, and that the process would be managed from the top. The question was whether it could be managed at all. The history of Africa's long-ruling liberation movements was not encouraging. From Zimbabwe to South Africa, from Tanzania to Mozambique, the transition from founding father to institutional rule had rarely been smooth. The founders, by their nature, had been creators rather than inheritors; they had built systems around their own energies, and when the energies faded, the systems often faded with them. Museveni, characteristically, believed he could defy the pattern. He had defied so much else. He had defied the predictions of collapse that accompanied his bush war; he had defied the economic orthodoxy of the 1980s; he had defied the wave of democratization that swept Africa in the 1990s without being washed away by it; he had defied the AIDS epidemic, the Lord's Resistance Army, the cascade of crises in the Great Lakes region. In the twilight of his rule, he seemed genuinely convinced that he could also defy time itself — not in the sense of living forever, but in the sense of so completely structuring the institutions of the state that they would continue to function, and to function well, after he was gone. The cabinet he appointed after the 2026 election was remarkable for its stability. The finance minister remained, a technocrat with international credibility. The foreign minister remained, a canny operator of the old school. The NRM secretariat remained, its leadership a blend of the party's founders and its new generation. What was notable was what was absent: fresh blood in the senior ranks. The President had chosen continuity over renewal, and in that choice lay the deepest irony of his final period. For the man who had come to power as an apostle of change had, in his closing act, become its principal obstacle. The young population that surged through Kampala's streets — underemployed, connected, ambitious — looked at the gerontocracy that governed them and felt not gratitude but impatience. The literacy rates that Museveni had championed had produced a generation that could read his speeches but could not find work worthy of their education. The highways he had built led to ports and borders but did not, for most, lead to prosperity. The peace he had secured had given the country a rare stability, but stability without opportunity began, in time, to feel like stagnation. The President understood this. In his calmer moments — the moments he shared with his diary, or with the small circle of advisors who had known him since the war — he confessed the dilemma. "The people want two things," he said once, in a private meeting, "and these two things conflict. They want a government that provides for them. And they want a government that leaves them alone. I have spent my life trying to give them one, but they will not forgive me for the other." The conflict between provision and freedom was not new. It had been the central tension of his rule from the beginning. The NRM had been born in a context — the chaos of the late 1970s and early 1980s — in which the provision of basic security had required the suspension of political rights. The movement had justified this suspension as temporary, a necessary stage before the establishment of a democratic system responsive to African conditions. But the temporary had become permanent, and the permanent had become comfortable. The democracy that Museveni had promised — the "democracy of the movement," over which he had presided instead of a party system — had morphed into something that looked, from the outside, exactly like the one-party rule he had condemned in Obote. The difference, as his defenders pointed out, was that it worked. The roads were built. The schools were opened. The army did not terrorize the population. The economy grew. But working, in the political sense, is a low bar, and the President had always believed in higher measures for himself. What, then, was the final accounting? The historians of the future would have a rich record to draw upon, and they would disagree, as historians always do. There would be those who would focus on the bodies — the casualties of Operation North in the 1980s, the toll of the Luwero Triangle, the war in the east of the Congo where Ugandan troops had marauded with impunity in the late 1990s, the suppression of dissent that had never quite been civilized. There would be those who focused on the survivors — the AIDS patients who lived because of his government's early and pragmatic response, the girls who went to school because of universal primary education, the entrepreneurs who built fortunes in the liberalized economy, the refugees who found a haven in Uganda while the rest of the world closed its doors. Both accounts would be true, and neither would be complete. The man was too large, and his era too long, for a single verdict. In the months that followed the election, the President threw himself into the final battles of his life. The first was the oil. The Tilenga and Kingfisher fields in the Albertine Graben, discovered in 2006, had taken two decades to bring to production — a saga of international negotiations, environmental opposition, pipeline politics, and the ordinary bureaucratic friction that attended any large enterprise in Uganda. The East African Crude Oil Pipeline, running 1,443 kilometres from Hoima to the Indian Ocean port of Tanga in Tanzania, had been one of the most contested infrastructure projects in the region's history. Its opponents included climate activists, human rights organisations, and the communities through which it would pass; its proponents included two governments, three multinational corporations, and the presidents themselves. Museveni had staked enormous personal authority on the pipeline's completion, and its first oil was now scheduled, with the usual slippage, for the end of the decade. "This is the harvest," he said, cutting the ribbon on yet another section of pipe. "We have sown for forty years. The harvest is coming." The crowd cheered, and the old man beamed, and for a moment it was possible to believe that the long wait had been worth it. The second battle was regional. The disorders of the Great Lakes had entered a new phase, one that was less dramatic than the catastrophes of the 1990s but no less consequential. The Democratic Republic of the Congo, perpetually fragile, had descended again into armed chaos in its eastern provinces, and Uganda had intervened — first in the joint operations against the Allied Democratic Forces, whose jihadist insurgency had spread terror across the border, and later in the broader regional efforts to stabilize the Kivus. The intervention was portrayed by Kampala as a necessary response to a clear threat; the Congolese public, scarred by the 1998-2003 war, was deeply suspicious. The Ugandan army had, once again, been accused of looting and abuse. The President's response was characteristically blunt. "We are not in the Congo for our health," he said. "We are there because the instability of the Congo is the instability of Uganda. We cannot have a burning house next to our own." It was the same argument he had made in 1979, when he had marched alongside Tanzanian troops to overthrow Amin, and in 1986, when he had taken Kampala, and in every crisis since. The argument was sound. The practice, as always, had been messier. The third battle was the most intimate — the battle to define his own legacy. The President had always been a reader, and in his final period he had returned to the books that had shaped him — the works of Nyerere and Nkrumah, the treatises on African liberation, the biographies of the founders of other revolutions. He had spoken with increasing frequency about the need for Africa to chart its own course, to reject the impositions of Western donors, to build institutions suited to its own conditions. The rhetoric was not new, but the emphasis was different. It had the quality of a man writing his own eulogy, and editing the historical record to suit his preferred version of events. He had taken to correcting the journalists who interviewed him, providing his own gloss on events that others remembered differently. He had commissioned histories of the bush war under his control. He had spoken, on several occasions, of the "correctness" of the NRM's liberation struggle, using the word with a frequency that suggested he had been reading more than African biographies — that somewhere, in the deep recesses of his private library, the ghosts of Moscow had reappeared, if only in the working vocabulary of an old man who had never quite abandoned the certainties of his youth. For the country, the spectacle was at once familiar and strange. The familiar part was the President's continuing presence, his continuing vigour, his continuing refusal to state what everyone knew — that the end was approaching, that the seasons were turning, that the hour had come for him to decide what he would leave behind. The strange part was the sense, in the air, that the decision might already have been made — not by him, but by the forces he had set in motion. The movement he had founded forty years ago had become an institution; the institution had become a culture; the culture had become a system. And the system, whatever its flaws, had achieved the thing that all regimes ultimately seek: the capacity to reproduce itself without the active participation of its founder. The young soldiers, the young ministers, the young members of parliament — they did not remember the bush war. They had grown up in the peace it had brought. They had attended the schools it had built. They had applied for the jobs it had created. They had learned the vocabulary of liberation without inheriting its conviction. And they would, in time, inherit the power. Whether that inheritance would be smooth was the final question. The President had one more election in him, if he wanted it, and no legal barrier stood in his way. The opposition had been reduced to a rump, and the international community had largely made its peace with the situation. The economy, while unequal, was growing; the region, while troubled, was not in flames; the state, while imperfect, was functional. The conditions for a transition were, by the standards of Africa, unusually favourable. What was lacking was the one thing that the President could not provide: the certainty that the transition would respect the principles — democratic, accountable, lawful — that he had spent his tenure undermining. The institutions he had built were strong enough to govern; they were not strong enough to substitute for his will. And so the country waited, as it had so often waited, for the old man to make his next move. It came, with the irony that had marked so much of his career, in the form of a speech in Parliament. The address, delivered on the fortieth anniversary of his taking power, was framed as a reflection on a historic milestone. But the attentive listeners — and there were few Ugandans who were not attentive that day — noticed something unusual. The President, for once, did not speak at length. The speech was brief, almost austere, by his standards. He reviewed the years since 1986 in a paragraph, thanked the comrades and the country, and then turned to the future. "It is not for me to choose what comes after me," he said. "It is for the institutions of this country, and for God, and for the people. I have done what I could. I have built what I could. I have served as long as I could. The rest is in other hands." He paused, and the silence in the chamber was absolute. "I have never feared the future," he said. "I have feared only that we would not be ready for it. I believe we are ready now." No announcement. No retirement. No succession plan announced. But the words were read, in every editorial and every radio program, as the closest thing to a valedictory that the old lion was capable of offering. The country drew a collective breath. The long afternoon was ending. The evening, with all its uncertainties and all its possibilities, was about to begin. ---
